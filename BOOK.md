## Preface — Become the author of the argument

Why does a copier need a loop around a successful write? What information must survive when a message arrives in several pieces? Why can a pipe reader wait forever even after the program that produced its input has exited? These questions have answers you can derive from a small set of contracts and an accounting of state.

This book teaches systems programming through that work. You will start with a problem and constraints, identify what must remain true, compare possible designs, and construct a case that defeats an incomplete explanation. Only then will you inspect an implementation. The goal is to make your design decisions explainable, including the conditions under which you would change them.

You already know basic C: functions, loops, structures, pointers, and simple allocation. The thirteen chapters develop user-space systems programming on macOS and Linux: memory, file I/O, filesystem objects, processes, pipes, signals, mappings, threads, sockets, event loops, debugging, and a complete search tool. Kernel implementation, device drivers, and embedded hardware remain outside this volume.

# A method for reasoning from first principles

Suppose someone asks you to build a fast message queue. It is tempting to reach for a familiar implementation, or to search for a clever one. Begin earlier: what does fast mean, which messages must survive, who produces and consumes them, and what happens when the consumer cannot keep up?

First-principles reasoning in this book means making your starting points explicit and building an argument from them. Some starting points are requirements. Others are mathematical facts, language rules, operating-system contracts, or assumptions you have chosen for a model. You then derive consequences, compare designs, and test the uncertain parts.

In a 2013 interview, Elon Musk describes first-principles reasoning as starting with fundamental truths and building conclusions from them. He also explains that the effort makes it worth applying selectively to important problems. That is the habit we will practice at consequential design decisions throughout this book. [Elon Musk interviewed by Alaina G. Levine, APS News](https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2013/10/profiles-in-versatility)

For systems programming, the exercise is concrete: question an inherited requirement, identify the facts that constrain a solution, estimate necessary work, and build a small experiment around the uncertain step. The derivations below are our applications of that habit. Their justification comes from the argument, contracts, and evidence you can inspect.

## Start at a useful boundary

You cannot derive a particular POSIX function signature from physics. People designed that interface. Nor can an experiment showing that invalid C code happened to work replace the language's lifetime rules. Good reasoning accepts a verified contract at the appropriate level and reasons carefully above it.

Choose a boundary that serves the problem. While designing a copier, take the documented behavior of `read` and `write` as inputs. While investigating an unexpected syscall cost, look below that boundary. While choosing a network protocol, the byte-stream contract matters; the precise voltage on a cable usually does not.

You can always ask another “why.” Useful stopping points are a relevant contract, a demonstrated mathematical consequence, or an explicitly uncertain assumption with a plan to investigate it. Writing a familiar term such as “the kernel handles it” is not a stopping point if the unresolved behavior is exactly what your design depends on.

## Keep a ledger of claims

Classify a claim before relying on it. The categories below prevent an observation from silently turning into a guarantee.

| Kind of claim | Example | How to challenge or establish it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Requirement | A successful copy must preserve every input byte in order | Ask the caller what success must mean |
| External contract | A successful write can report less progress than requested | Read the relevant interface specification and platform notes |
| Model assumption | The file is not modified during this scan | State who can ensure it, or design for changes |
| Mathematical consequence | A finite buffer cannot hold an indefinitely growing backlog | State the model and follow its accounting |
| Observation | This run made three positive reads | Record the input, environment, and trace |
| Design choice | Use an 8192-byte input buffer | Compare consequences under the workload |

Requirements can change, models can be incomplete, and implementations can have bugs. Labeling a claim does not protect it from criticism; it tells you what kind of criticism is relevant. A benchmark can compare buffer sizes. It cannot establish that a caller will never truncate a mapped file.

For an important assumption, record the consequence if it is false. “There is one producer” is incomplete documentation for a concurrent structure. Add which state transitions would conflict if a second producer appeared and how the interface enforces or exposes that restriction.

## Question a requirement that already names the solution

“Make this queue ten times larger” sounds like a requirement. It may actually be a proposed remedy for lost records. Ask what outcome the caller needs: tolerate a finite burst, reduce producer waiting, or guarantee delivery during an outage? Each answer leads to a different problem.

Classify the constraints before accepting the remedy. Finite storage is a resource limit. Delivering every accepted record is a promise. A particular queue capacity is a choice. The belief that outages last at most two seconds is an assumption requiring evidence. A larger queue may satisfy the burst requirement while leaving sustained overload untouched.

Then ask which work can disappear while preserving the agreed result. If a display needs only the latest reading, retaining every intermediate reading may be unnecessary. If the recorder promises an audit history, deleting readings violates the result. Changing a requirement needs agreement from whoever depends on it; discovering an inconvenient cost does not authorize silently weakening the promise.

Use this question at a design boundary: **Which requirement would fail if this component, copy, allocation, or synchronization step disappeared?** Give a concrete input or event sequence as the answer. If you cannot, investigate whether the step is necessary before making it faster. If you can, retain the obligation and compare other ways to fulfill it.

## Estimate the floor before tuning the implementation

A cost model separates work forced by the result from work introduced by a design. Consider a program receiving an arbitrary byte stream and forwarding every byte unchanged. For `N` input bytes, `N` bytes must reach the destination under that contract. Staging the entire input in another file adds a storage requirement that forwarding alone does not impose. It may still be worthwhile for replay or recovery, but those benefits need their own requirements.

Write units beside every estimate: bytes retained, calls made, records per second, or seconds waiting. Label idealized bounds and measured values separately. A lower bound identifies work a design cannot avoid under its model; it does not promise an implementation can reach that bound. System-call costs, scheduling, failure handling, and interactions between stages can account for a gap.

This gives optimization a direction: remove work whose purpose has disappeared, simplify what remains, then measure the suspected limit. In the following chapters, apply the question to intermediate buffers, parent processes, shared counters, and network framing. Reuse familiar designs when they meet the contract; reopen their assumptions when a changed workload or requirement makes the cost matter.

## A repeatable reasoning cycle

Use the following sequence while studying and designing. It is a tool for exposing missing reasoning, not a form you must mechanically fill out for every line of code.

1. **Define the result.** State observable inputs, outputs, success, failure, and resource limits. Replace adjectives such as fast, safe, and robust with questions you can evaluate. If a request prescribes a mechanism, ask which outcome requires it.
2. **State the starting facts.** Separate requirements, external contracts, assumptions, and observations. Investigate a contract when uncertainty about it could invalidate your design.
3. **Find the state that must survive.** Ask what information a future decision needs. Distinguish owned resources from borrowed access.
4. **Write an invariant and a progress argument.** Identify what must remain true at each boundary and what permits the next step to happen.
5. **Compare plausible designs.** Evaluate at least two against the same constraints. Estimate unavoidable work and identify work each design adds. An alternative can be correct yet have an unsuitable cost or contract.
6. **Construct a counterexample.** Break an assumption, choose an awkward boundary, or interrupt progress. Predict the result before executing anything.
7. **Test and revise.** Use proof for consequences of a model and experiments for claims about an implementation or environment. Preserve failures as tests.
8. **Transfer the idea.** Change one requirement and work out which conclusions still hold. This distinguishes an understood argument from a memorized implementation.

The state step is often decisive. A partial write forces you to remember progress somewhere, but it does not force a particular variable name or data structure. A delayed callback forces a decision about who retains the unaccepted event. The purpose of the derivation is to expose necessary information and tradeoffs, not to pretend there is only one possible program.

## Worked derivation: a recorder with a slow consumer

Consider a recorder receiving fixed-size records from a producer and forwarding them to a consumer. Ignore threads initially. Treat production and consumption as events that may occur in either order.

The requested properties are:

- Every accepted record must eventually be delivered, in acceptance order, if the consumer continues making progress.
- Storage for pending records is finite: at most `B` records.
- Initially no records are pending.
- The consumer may temporarily be slower than the producer.

These statements still omit a decisive policy: can the producer wait, or can an attempted record be rejected? Before choosing a queue representation, examine whether the requested behavior is possible.

Let `A(t)` be the number of records accepted by time `t` and `D(t)` the number delivered. With no loss or duplication, the number pending is:

```text
Q(t) = A(t) - D(t)
0 <= Q(t) <= B
```

This is bookkeeping under the stated model, not a performance measurement. If the producer must accept every attempted record immediately while the consumer may stop indefinitely, eventually `A - D` can exceed any finite `B`. Those requirements cannot all be maintained. A better data structure cannot make the contradiction disappear.

To see the pressure numerically, use a hypothetical constant-rate approximation: attempts arrive at 1000 records per second, the consumer delivers 800 per second, and storage holds 2000 records. If every attempt is accepted, the backlog grows by approximately 200 records per second and fills the buffer in about ten seconds. Bursts, scheduling, and discrete events change the trajectory; these numbers are an illustrative model, not results from the book's programs.

Changing a requirement creates several possible designs:

| Policy when full | What it preserves | What the caller must accept |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Wait for space | Every completed acceptance can be delivered | Producer latency may grow without a finite bound |
| Reject the new record | Existing accepted records remain intact | Caller handles rejection, retry, or loss before acceptance |
| Evict an old record | Recent records can continue arriving | The original promise to every accepted record changes |
| Spill to another store | A larger temporary backlog may be retained | Additional storage, latency, and failure behavior enter the contract |

Spilling is not infinite storage. It changes the resource budget and its failure cases. Likewise, retrying rejected records somewhere else merely moves a queue unless the caller also has a bound and an overload policy.

Choose waiting for this version. Our operation `push` completes successfully only after inserting a record. A blocked attempt has not yet been accepted. Now derive a representation.

An array with `B` slots avoids allocating per record. A bounded pool of linked nodes can also satisfy the contract. The array has simple contiguous storage; the pool makes some pointer-based operations convenient but adds links and lifetime bookkeeping. Nothing in the requirement makes the array the only correct answer. We select it because this queue needs only insertion at one end and removal at the other.

Let `head` identify the next record to remove, and let `count` be the number pending. For positive capacity, maintain:

```text
0 <= head < B
0 <= count <= B
the logical queue occupies count slots starting at head, wrapping at B
```

The next insertion position is mathematically `(head + count) mod B`. In C, the implementation must also justify the integer arithmetic; mathematical addition and machine addition are different contracts. A bounded capacity and checked arithmetic, or an equivalent branch that avoids overflow, can make the representation valid.

At empty, `count == 0`; at full, `count == B`. A design storing only equal head and tail positions cannot distinguish those two states without another rule. It could reserve a slot, store a count, or add a phase bit. The apparent implementation detail is a consequence of needing to distinguish two observable states.

Now add multiple threads. A producer cannot expose the incremented count before the inserted record is ready for a consumer, and two producers cannot independently claim the same slot. The representation requires a coordinated state transition. A mutex is one way to enforce it; a more specialized atomic algorithm needs its own proof. Calling the queue lock-free does not supply that proof.

## Safety does not imply progress

Suppose every queue operation holds the right mutex, yet a producer waits while holding that mutex until a consumer removes an item. No consumer can acquire the mutex to create space. Bounds may remain intact while the system makes no progress.

Separate two questions:

- **Safety:** can anything prohibited happen, such as reading an uninitialized slot or delivering an item twice?
- **Progress:** under the stated environment assumptions, can a waiting operation eventually proceed or terminate?

Progress claims need assumptions. If the consumer stops forever and the policy is to wait for space, an insertion may wait forever by design. If the system includes shutdown, specify which waiters it wakes and what happens to already accepted work. A claim of eventual completion that omits these conditions is stronger than the design supports.

In a condition-variable implementation, the queue state answers whether progress is permitted. Notifications invite a waiter to recheck that state. This is why the Chapter 9 derivation begins with a predicate and lifetime argument rather than treating a notification as a stored work item.

## Design an experiment that could change your mind

Before running a program, write a prediction and an observation that would challenge it. “The test should pass” is too weak. “After closing the queue, both blocked producers return rejection and accepted items are drained” names observable events and a contract.

Use tiny cases to expose mechanisms. Capacity one forces empty/full transitions. A payload split after the third header byte challenges parsing. Leaving one pipe writer open challenges a reader's termination. A failed allocation challenges whether the old object remains owned.

An informative experiment often distinguishes two explanations. If a consumer is waiting, it might have no input, or a producer might still own an unused write descriptor. A log of bytes produced alone cannot distinguish those states. A descriptor-ownership trace and a controlled closure can.

Do not claim an experiment proved more than it exercised. A successful run can support the correctness of a path. It cannot prove the absence of undefined behavior on every path, or guarantee a different operating system's behavior. Conversely, a counterexample can be enough to disprove a universal claim.

Performance experiments need the same discipline. Specify a workload and a hypothesis, choose a measurement boundary, preserve the observable result, and identify confounders. A lower elapsed time does not by itself prove fewer cache misses. Chapter 12 develops that reasoning with reproducible measurement tasks.

## Know when to revise and when to stop

Review the central design in a chapter using the six questions below. This is the book's review standard, informed by the published first-principles approach discussed above; it is not a test supplied by Elon Musk or evidence of his approval.

| Review question | Evidence the explanation must supply |
| --- | --- |
| What result is actually needed? | Observable success, failure, and resource limits, before selecting a mechanism. |
| What supports the starting claims? | Requirements, documented contracts, assumptions, and observations kept distinct. |
| What cannot be removed under those claims? | Necessary information or work, an invariant, and the conditions for progress. Quantified costs need units and a stated model. |
| What could be removed or replaced? | A simpler candidate and the exact requirement it preserves or violates. |
| What could defeat this explanation? | A concrete counterexample or an experiment that distinguishes competing explanations, with a prediction made first. |
| What would make the decision change? | A changed requirement or assumption and the resulting revision to the argument. |

Mark each answer **supported**, **conditional**, or **missing**, and point to its evidence. A conditional conclusion must name its assumption and what fails without it. Missing support blocks the corresponding claim; strong answers elsewhere do not compensate for it. When the subject is object identity or lifetime, deriving necessary state is more useful than inventing a timing formula. When the subject is performance, a plausible story needs a cost model and measurements before it becomes a speed claim.

For example, a proposed explanation that says “mapping removes explicit reads, so it must be faster” fails this review. It has not accounted for page-fault work or stated a workload and measurement boundary. A defensible revision compares mapping and buffered reading on the same stable input, checks equal counts, predicts which work changes, and measures it. Until then, the performance advantage is a hypothesis. Chapter 8 supplies the contracts and alternatives; Chapter 12 supplies the experiment discipline.

Apply the same standard to a design suggested by an expert: identify which premises and evidence support it. Fix a discovered defect, then revisit the affected reasoning. A review passes when all six questions have adequate answers within the stated scope, including clearly bounded conditional claims.

This stopping rule does not establish that no future refinement exists. A new workload, platform, or requirement can expose a missing case. Record unresolved uncertainty separately from finished work so that it remains visible without making every task endless.

## Derive it yourself

A monitoring display must retain the latest 100 accepted readings. It may discard older readings, and producers must never wait for the display to read them. Assume the reading operation and insertion are serialized for this exercise. Define the behavior of the 101st insertion, derive the state you need, and compare two representations. Identify which requirement differs from the lossless queue above.

Then change exactly one assumption: a display reader may still be using a returned pointer when an insertion arrives. Does your previous ownership argument survive? Write a concrete sequence of operations that forces a choice about copying, pinning, or synchronization.

# Chapter 1 — Programs become processes

A test harness launches the same executable twice. One invocation should succeed; the other should deliberately return a chosen failure status. A person wants a readable account of each run, while the harness needs a result it can interpret without reading English. Design a command for both callers.

Call it `identity [STATUS]`. It should report its process and account identifiers, its argument list, and the requested status. It accepts at most one optional decimal status from 0 through 125. Invalid arguments produce a diagnostic and status 2. A detected output error takes precedence over the requested status and produces status 1. Otherwise the command returns the requested status, defaulting to zero. This is a small program with several observable promises, not simply a request to print some numbers.

## Decide what the environment gives you

You cannot derive an operating-system interface from a preference for simple designs. You choose an environment, learn its contracts, then reason within them. Here the environment is C17 with a common POSIX interface set on macOS and Linux.

| Statement | What kind of knowledge is it? |
| --- | --- |
| The runtime supplies arguments to `main`. | An external language/runtime contract. |
| A process has an identifier obtainable through a POSIX interface. | An external operating-system contract. |
| Repeated runs on your machine printed increasing identifiers. | An observation, not a promised ordering. |
| Invalid input returns 2, and a detected output failure returns 1. | Our command's design choices. |
| Standard output is connected to a usable destination. | An environmental assumption to test, not silently promote into a guarantee. |

A program describes computation. An executable file contains code and information needed to run it on a particular platform. A process is an instance with memory, execution state, and operating-system resources. Running one executable twice creates separate instances whose arguments and connections can differ. This distinction explains why recompiling is unnecessary to request a different exit status.

The kernel manages shared resources and enforces access between processes. Ordinary C code runs in user space. A library interface may enter the kernel, perform work locally, or combine both. Formatting an integer is library work; delivering the resulting characters eventually involves the operating system. One C function call does not imply one system call.

## Compare two interfaces before implementing either

One plausible design prints `success` or `failure` and always exits zero. A human can read it, but the harness must now parse text. If a diagnostic changes language or wording, the harness can break. Worse, an output failure can erase the only result. This design does not satisfy our machine-readable completion requirement.

A second design returns a status and prints everything to the same output stream. That gives the harness a result, but a caller saving normal output also saves argument-error messages. A consumer expecting records must distinguish data from diagnostics.

We choose three channels: normal records on standard output, diagnostics on standard error, and a bounded exit status for completion. That choice follows from our callers' needs; another application could legitimately choose a structured message protocol instead. Separating channels does not ensure that every message reaches a human. If the diagnostic destination fails too, the command has no additional guaranteed reporting channel.

The shell connects these channels before our code runs. Therefore argument validation cannot protect a preexisting file from a shell redirection that already truncated it. Keep responsibility with the component that performs the action.

Suppose someone adds a requirement: “Write a result file so the harness can discover whether the run succeeded.” Separate the desired fact from the proposed mechanism. Does the harness need a completion status immediately, or a record it can recover after restarting? For the first need, the existing exit status may be sufficient. A result file would introduce naming, ownership, stale-record detection, and cleanup without necessarily supplying new information. For the second need, an exit status alone is insufficient; persistence is a real additional requirement.

Test the uncertain assumption with a small harness that discards normal output, waits for the command, and records its status immediately. Run requests for zero and seven. If the caller can distinguish them, text parsing and a result file are unnecessary for that particular decision. This experiment tests the caller's capabilities; it does not decide whether persistent history matters to its users.

## Derive the minimum state and error precedence

The only persistent decision is the requested status. Parsing also needs a temporary result and evidence about which input characters were consumed. Process identifiers can be retrieved and printed without retaining a history of earlier runs. Keeping such a history would add state without serving this contract.

Our control flow has three phases: validate, report, complete. The invariant before reporting is that the status is representable and within our chosen range. The invariant during argument output is that only entries below `argc` are accessed. Completion must select the strongest detected outcome: invalid syntax prevents reporting; a later detected output failure overrides an otherwise valid requested status.

Consider an apparently convenient conversion that maps unrecognized text to zero. Both `0` and `nonsense` could then lead to success. Information lost at conversion cannot be recovered by a later range check. We need separate evidence for whether conversion started, where it stopped, and whether its result was representable.

Safety is not the same as completion. A finite argument list gives a finite amount of formatting work, but a write to a blocked pipe can wait indefinitely. This program has no deadline. Its progress claim is conditional: if its library operations return, its bounded loops finish. Signal termination is another possible outcome; the reference retains the default signal policy, so its status-1 rule covers detected output errors, not every possible termination.

## Derive it yourself

Before opening the reference implementation, write a decision table for no argument, `7`, `7x`, an empty argument, and an integer far larger than a `long`. Add a row where parsing succeeds but the final output flush fails. State which phase runs and which status wins.

Then predict this sequence without running it: request status 7, run another successful command, and only then inspect `$?`. What information has been overwritten, and by which component? Finally, decide whether leading spaces, a plus sign, and trailing spaces belong in your input grammar. A convenient library parser must not make that decision invisibly.

## Map the design to documented interfaces

`getpid` and `getppid` obtain the process and parent identifiers. These identifiers describe current process relationships, not permanent identities across time. The [process identification manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/getpid.2.html) documents those interfaces and Linux-specific details. `getuid` and `getgid` report real account identifiers, not a complete authorization model.

The reference uses `strtol`. It checks a reported conversion error, an unchanged end pointer, trailing characters, and the accepted range. Its chosen grammar permits the leading whitespace and optional sign recognized by `strtol`, provided the numeric result is 0–125; it rejects trailing text, including trailing spaces. In particular, textual `-0` is accepted because its value is zero. A stricter decimal grammar would need explicit lexical validation.

Types such as `pid_t` and `uid_t` belong to the platform interface. Converting to `intmax_t` or `uintmax_t` and using the corresponding `j` format modifier gives the variadic print function the type it expects. A number that looks small does not justify passing the wrong argument type.

Returning from the initial invocation of `main` performs normal C termination. Normal termination flushes standard-I/O streams, but an explicit flush permits us to inspect failure before choosing a status. POSIX exposes a limited status to the waiting parent, not an arbitrary integer return value. The [exit manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/exit.3.html) describes these termination rules.

# Chapter 2 — Memory, bytes, and ownership

Design a command that accepts arbitrary bytes from standard input and reproduces them exactly on standard output, but emits no input bytes until collection has completed successfully. If reading or storage allocation fails, report failure and discard the collected data. Input length is not known in advance. This version may use growing memory, but no temporary file.

Those requirements explain why this chapter buffers an entire input. Streaming each chunk immediately would save memory, but would violate the decision to withhold output: if the third read failed, the first two chunks would already have escaped. We will change that requirement in Chapter 3. Do not mistake one chapter's chosen constraint for a universal recommendation to buffer files.

## Find the cost forced by the requirement

For arbitrary binary input, compression cannot guarantee a small representation of every possible input. If two different accepted inputs lead to exactly the same retained state, the program cannot later reconstruct both correctly from that state alone. Thus the amount of information retained must grow with the input in the worst case. Moving the information into a temporary file changes where the cost is paid; it does not eliminate it.

An unlimited-input promise also cannot guarantee success on a finite machine. Our contract must permit failure when storage or representable sizes are exhausted. This is a requirement correction, not an implementation defect. A program that silently truncates the input would be changing the requested result instead.

Within those constraints, compare two representations. A fixed array is simple and predictable but introduces a maximum accepted length. A linked list of chunks avoids moving earlier chunks, at the cost of per-chunk metadata and a less convenient contiguous view. A growable contiguous array gives us simple indexing and one allocation to own, but growth can relocate it. We choose the third representation to study that tradeoff; it is not the only correct design.

## Separate language facts from allocation policy

| Claim | Its status in this design |
| --- | --- |
| An object can be accessed only within its valid lifetime and permitted bounds. | An external C contract. |
| A failed positive-size `realloc` leaves the original allocation available. | An external allocation contract. |
| Capacity doubles when more space is needed. | Our growth policy, not an allocator guarantee. |
| A particular run grew an allocation without changing its printed address. | An observation that cannot justify retaining old pointers after successful reallocation. |
| Input buffers passed to append do not overlap our allocation. | An API precondition selected to simplify ownership. |

C describes objects, types, storage duration, and observable behavior. “Stack” and “heap” describe usual implementations, not mandatory physical regions. A local array generally has automatic storage duration; a file-scope object or local `static` has static storage duration. Allocated storage remains usable according to the allocation interface's lifetime rules. Copying a pointer does not extend the pointed-to object's lifetime.

We use ownership as a design agreement: one component must eventually release an allocation. Other components borrow access for a specified interval. C does not enforce that agreement, so control flow and interface contracts must make it reviewable.

## Derive what must be remembered

To reproduce the input, we need an allocation reference and the number of stored bytes. To reuse spare allocation space without repeatedly asking for more, we also retain capacity. Capacity is optimization state; correctness could instead use an exact-size allocation after each append.

Let `len` count stored bytes and `cap` count allocated byte slots. The invariant is `0 <= len <= cap`. Positive capacity means a live allocation of at least that size; our empty state uses zero capacity and a null pointer. The first `len` bytes contain exactly the accumulated input. Spare slots are not input, even if they happen to contain convenient values.

An append operation must preserve the existing prefix, copy the new suffix, and publish the new length only after that suffix exists. Reserving space may change the allocation but must not change the logical input. Destruction releases the allocation and returns the object to its empty state. These transitions explain the functions we need before we decide their names.

Why not recover length with `strlen`? An input consisting of bytes `41 00 42` contains three bytes. A string operation would treat the zero byte as an ending and lose the third byte. A byte sequence therefore needs its own length. It need not have a terminator at `data[len]`, which might be outside the allocation.

`sizeof` measures C bytes; `CHAR_BIT` reports their bit width. Our macOS and Linux targets have eight-bit bytes. `sizeof pointer` still measures the pointer object, not an allocation reached through it. Character types can inspect object representations, but raw structures do not become portable file formats: padding, widths, and byte order remain separate encoding decisions.

## Reject two convenient but invalid transitions

Suppose a growth attempt assigns its result directly to the only owning pointer. If allocation fails, that pointer becomes null while the original allocation still exists. The program has lost the reference needed to read or release it. A temporary result allows us to commit the new owner only after success.

Now suppose the program computes a required size by adding first and checking afterward. Use an imaginary unsigned size type whose maximum is 255. With `len = 250` and an append of 10 bytes, the sum wraps to 4. A request for four slots cannot make a ten-byte append at offset 250 safe. The inequality must be rearranged before evaluating the sum: reject when `n > SIZE_MAX - len`. For arrays of larger elements, check the element count against `SIZE_MAX / sizeof *data` before multiplying.

Unsigned wrapping is defined behavior, but it can still violate our contract. Signed overflow is undefined behavior. These allocation and arithmetic rules are documented in sections 6.2.5 and 7.22.3 of the [WG14 C11 committee draft](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg14/www/docs/n1570.pdf), background for the C17 subset used here. Language-defined does not mean application-correct.

## Account for work as well as safety

An exact-size growth request on every one-byte append may repeatedly move all previous bytes. Under a model where each growth relocates the buffer, copying costs sum as `1 + 2 + ... + (N - 1)`, which is quadratic. An allocator may sometimes extend in place, but our design cannot depend on that observation.

While capacity grows geometrically, previous capacities form a geometric series. Their sum is bounded by a constant multiple of the final capacity, so relocation copies total linear work in the accumulated input size under this model. The tradeoff is unused capacity. The reference starts at 64 and doubles, except that it checks for overflow before doubling and uses the required size when doubling would overflow. That fallback preserves arithmetic safety but can cause repeated exact-size growth near `SIZE_MAX`; the geometric-series bound does not cover that regime. This explains the policy without claiming a particular allocator's timing.

Termination has assumptions too. Each growth-loop iteration increases capacity or selects the final required size. Each input iteration consumes bytes, encounters an end, or reports a read failure. An endless source need not finish; finite available memory may be exhausted first. We have proved local progress, not a deadline or guaranteed success for every source.

# Chapter 3 — Files and partial I/O

Copy an input stream of unknown length to an output stream using a fixed amount of memory. This time output may begin before input finishes. Preserve every input byte, including zero bytes, in order. If an operation fails, report failure even if an output prefix has already escaped. The command accepts no filenames; its caller supplies the two connections.

That single change from Chapter 2 removes the need to remember the entire input. It does not remove the need to remember unfinished work. The central design question is now: when may the program safely reuse a buffer?

## Write down the conditions for the promise

Our program, `copyfd`, uses ordinary blocking byte-stream operations. It assumes usable standard descriptors and exclusive responsibility for their stream positions while copying. An unrelated writer sharing the output can interleave its own bytes; an unrelated reader sharing input can consume bytes we never see. The tool cannot promise an uncontaminated copy while another actor changes the very stream being copied.

| Statement | Its role |
| --- | --- |
| A successful transfer reports the number of bytes transferred, which may be short. | A POSIX interface contract. |
| The next request concerns bytes not yet transferred. | A consequence of our no-loss, no-duplication requirement. |
| The reusable array has 16384 bytes. | An implementation choice. |
| Descriptors use blocking behavior. | An assumption of this command's supported operating mode. |
| A successful call stored data durably on a physical device. | An unjustified inference; the transfer interface does not promise it. |

The target is the byte sequence observed and emitted through these interfaces. It is not a coherent snapshot of a concurrently modified file, nor a promise about a terminal's rendering of those bytes. Make the boundary of the claim explicit before testing it.

## Compare designs by the requirement they satisfy

A whole-input buffer preserves bytes but fails our fixed-memory requirement for arbitrarily long input. A one-byte buffer satisfies it and simplifies bookkeeping, at the cost of many operations. A larger reusable buffer has the same logical behavior while reducing calls when endpoints permit larger transfers. We choose the third, without pretending that 16384 is a universal optimum.

There is also a choice of abstraction. C's buffered stream interface can implement a correct copier. POSIX descriptor operations expose transfer counts more directly, making incomplete progress easier to study. This chapter uses that interface for the data path and standard I/O for diagnostics on a separate stream. Neither family of functions is intrinsically more correct.

A tempting larger-buffer implementation performs one read, one write of everything read, then repeats. Suppose the read supplies ten bytes but the write accepts three. Reusing the buffer immediately loses seven bytes. Retrying the original ten instead duplicates the accepted prefix: after `ABC`, the next output begins `ABC` again. These are different failures caused by the same missing fact—how far the output operation progressed.

## Derive state from conservation of bytes

For one chunk, store `len`, the valid byte count, and `done`, the count already emitted. The pending region is `[done, len)`. Its invariant is `0 <= done <= len <= capacity`, and it must remain unchanged until those pending bytes have been emitted or the operation has failed.

Conceptually, during successful copying, total bytes read equal total bytes emitted plus bytes still pending. These totals are useful reasoning variables; the implementation need not store ever-growing counters. The bounded pair `len` and `done` contains enough information for the next action.

A successful output of `n` bytes changes only `done`, increasing it by `n`. When `done == len`, there are no pending bytes, so the array can safely receive the next input chunk. The program must not read into that array earlier. This is the ownership rule connecting the two loops.

Separate safety from progress. Bounds ensure that an output request refers to live bytes in the pending region. For progress, use `len - done` as a decreasing measure: each positive write reduces it. An interrupted call with no reported progress leaves it unchanged. A zero result for a nonempty request cannot support either advancement or an endless retry, so the implementation treats it as an error.

The proof is conditional. A blocking operation may wait indefinitely, and a repeated interruption need not eventually stop. This program has no overall deadline. “Every positive result decreases remaining work” is a useful statement; “this loop always finishes quickly” would be unsupported.

## Read the result before classifying the event

A file descriptor is a nonnegative integer naming a process-local reference to an operating-system-managed endpoint. For an ordinary file, the underlying open file description carries state such as the offset. Copying the integer into another variable does not create a new descriptor. The standard descriptor constants are `STDIN_FILENO`, `STDOUT_FILENO`, and `STDERR_FILENO`.

A C `FILE *` also carries library stream state and buffering. Mixing buffered operations and descriptor operations on the same connection requires coordination because a library stream may already hold data the descriptor-level code cannot see. Our data path avoids that additional state.

For a positive-size `read`, a positive result is the count obtained and minus one denotes failure. This copier treats zero as the end of its input, as appropriate for regular files, pipes, and ordinary connected stream sockets. A short success is not itself EOF. These return rules are documented in the [read manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/read.2.html). Store the result in `ssize_t`; converting a negative failure result to `size_t` before testing it destroys the distinction between count and error.

Do not generalize that zero-result interpretation to every device configuration. A noncanonical terminal with `VMIN = 0` can return zero because no byte arrived before its timer expired, even with `O_NONBLOCK` clear. Such terminal modes need a different input policy and are outside this copier's contract. [Terminal read modes](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/termios.3.html)

Only a reported failure gives `errno` its relevant meaning. Successful calls need not reset it. A stale `EINTR` after a positive count must not cause a retry that discards progress. If cleanup occurs before reporting a failure, save the original error first; another operation may overwrite it.

For this copier, `read` or `write` reporting `-1` with `EINTR` is retried without changing the offset. Other failures end the copy. A nonblocking descriptor may report `EAGAIN` or `EWOULDBLOCK`; that is outside this version's supported mode and is reported as failure. Repeating immediately would waste CPU rather than establish readiness.

Positive output counts mean accepted bytes, not durable storage or completed processing by another application. The [write manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/write.2.html) describes partial transfers and delayed errors. Durability would add different requirements and operations to our design.

        ssize_t n = read(STDIN_FILENO, data, sizeof data);
        if (n > 0) {
            if (write_all(STDOUT_FILENO, data, (size_t)n) == -1) {
                perror("copyfd: write");
                result = 1;
                break;
            }
        } else if (n == 0) {
            break;
        } else if (errno != EINTR) {
            perror("copyfd: read");
            result = 1;
            break;
        }
    }

    /* These are one-shot closes, not EINTR retry loops. */
    if (close(STDOUT_FILENO) == -1) {
        perror("copyfd: close stdout");
        result = 1;
    }
    if (close(STDIN_FILENO) == -1) {
        perror("copyfd: close stdin");
        result = 1;
    }
    return result;
}
```

The small signal setup makes a broken output pipe observable through an error return by ignoring `SIGPIPE`. Otherwise the default signal action normally terminates the writer. That is explicit process policy, explained in Chapter 7, rather than part of the byte-conservation argument.

## Cleanup has a different contract

Do not generalize an I/O retry rule into “retry every interrupted system call.” A close operation releases a reference whose numeric identifier can be reused. If release already happened, a retry might act on a different resource assigned the same integer.

Interrupted-close behavior differs across standards editions and implementations. Linux releases the descriptor even when reporting `EINTR`, whereas POSIX.1-2024 specifies different behavior. The [close manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/close.2.html) explains this divergence. Our short-lived command attempts each close once, reports failure, and exits; termination releases any remaining descriptors. This is a stated policy for this program, not a portable resource-management recipe for a long-lived library.

The command returns zero only after successful copying and its checked closes; detected operational failures return 1 and arguments are rejected with 2. A failure may leave an output prefix. Status communicates that the copy did not complete; it cannot retract bytes already accepted downstream. Even success does not establish durable storage.

# Chapter 4 — Names and filesystem objects

A pathname looks like the identity of a file because we use names to discuss files. The operating system's model is more subtle. A name is part of a directory structure that can change. An open descriptor refers to an object obtained through an earlier operation. Understanding that distinction explains hard links, symbolic links, renaming, and a large class of race conditions.

Our `fileinfo` tool reports metadata for one pathname. It distinguishes a symbolic link from its target and lists the immediate names inside a directory. It does not create, remove, or rename entries, or write file contents. Observation can still affect metadata: reading a directory can update its access timestamp. [POSIX directory-read semantics](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/readdir.3p.html)

## Separate a name from an object

A regular file contains bytes and associated metadata. A directory maps names to filesystem objects. The pathname `notes/chapter.txt` requests a sequence of lookups beginning at the current directory. An absolute pathname begins at the root directory of the process's filesystem view instead. `.` and `..` participate in pathname resolution; a pathname is more than a string with slashes removed.

A hard link adds another directory entry referring to the same object. Neither name is inherently the original. Removing one name does not remove the other. A symbolic link is a separate object containing a target pathname. Its target can be absent. When a relative symbolic-link target is followed, its interpretation begins at the directory containing the link, not at the caller's current directory.

The device and inode fields together help identify filesystem objects within the relevant filesystem view at an observation point. An inode number alone is insufficient across filesystems, and inode numbers can be reused over time. Treat a captured number as diagnostic information, not a durable global identifier.

## Ask the right metadata question

`stat(path, ...)` normally follows the final symbolic link and reports the target. `lstat(path, ...)` reports the final link itself when that component is a symbolic link. `fstat(fd, ...)` reports the object associated with an open descriptor. The [stat family manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/stat.2.html) documents these distinctions and metadata fields.

The word “final” matters. `lstat` does not prohibit symbolic links in earlier pathname components. Nor is it a complete defense against concurrent pathname changes. It answers a particular metadata question at a particular operation.

The program initially calls `lstat`. For a symbolic link, it also calls `stat` and labels the two results separately. For a directory, it opens a directory stream and calls `fstat` on that stream's descriptor. That second result describes the directory actually opened, even if the pathname changed after the initial lookup.

Metadata is interpreted according to object type. For a regular file, `st_size` is the logical byte length. A directory's size is not its number of entries. Use `S_ISREG`, `S_ISDIR`, and related predicates to inspect `st_mode`; do not compare the entire mode value to a file-type constant, because the value also includes other bits.

```c
#include <dirent.h>
#include <errno.h>
#include <inttypes.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <sys/stat.h>
#include <unistd.h>

static const char *kind(mode_t mode)
{
    if (S_ISREG(mode)) return "regular";
    if (S_ISDIR(mode)) return "directory";
    if (S_ISLNK(mode)) return "symlink";
    if (S_ISFIFO(mode)) return "fifo";
    if (S_ISSOCK(mode)) return "socket";
    if (S_ISCHR(mode)) return "character-device";
    if (S_ISBLK(mode)) return "block-device";
    return "other";
}

static void show_stat(const char *label, const struct stat *st)
{
    printf("%s: type=%s device=%ju inode=%ju links=%ju size=%jd\n",
           label, kind(st->st_mode), (uintmax_t)st->st_dev,
           (uintmax_t)st->st_ino, (uintmax_t)st->st_nlink,
           (intmax_t)st->st_size);
}

/* Escape bytes so newline and terminal-control bytes stay visible. */
static void show_name(const char *name)
{
    putchar('"');
    for (const unsigned char *p = (const unsigned char *)name; *p; ++p) {
        if (*p >= 32 && *p <= 126 && *p != '\\' && *p != '"') {
            putchar(*p);
        } else {
            printf("\\x%02x", (unsigned int)*p);
        }
    }
    puts("\"");
}

static int list_directory(const char *path)
{
    DIR *dir = opendir(path);
    if (dir == NULL) {
        perror("fileinfo: opendir");
        return 1;
    }
    int result = 0;
    struct stat opened;
    if (fstat(dirfd(dir), &opened) == -1) {
        perror("fileinfo: fstat");
        result = 1;
    } else {
        show_stat("opened-directory", &opened);
        for (;;) {
            errno = 0;
            struct dirent *entry = readdir(dir);
            if (entry == NULL) {
                if (errno != 0) {
                    perror("fileinfo: readdir");
                    result = 1;
                }
                break;
            }
            if (strcmp(entry->d_name, ".") != 0 &&
                strcmp(entry->d_name, "..") != 0) {
                show_name(entry->d_name);
            }
        }
    }
    if (closedir(dir) == -1) {
        perror("fileinfo: closedir");
        result = 1;
    }
    return result;
}

int main(int argc, char **argv)
{
    if (argc != 2) {
        fprintf(stderr, "usage: fileinfo PATH\n");
        return 2;
    }
    struct stat named;
    if (lstat(argv[1], &named) == -1) {
        perror("fileinfo: lstat");
        return 1;
    }
    show_stat("name", &named);
    int result = 0;
    if (S_ISLNK(named.st_mode)) {
        struct stat target;
        if (stat(argv[1], &target) == -1) {
            perror("fileinfo: stat target");
            result = 1;
        } else {
            show_stat("target", &target);
        }
    } else if (S_ISDIR(named.st_mode)) {
        result = list_directory(argv[1]);
    }
    if (fflush(stdout) == EOF || ferror(stdout)) {
        perror("fileinfo: stdout");
        result = 1;
    }
    return result;
}
```

## Directory iteration is a separate protocol

`opendir` creates a `DIR *` stream. Repeated `readdir` calls yield entries. The returned pointer is borrowed from the stream implementation; consume or copy the name before later operations invalidate or replace it. Finish with `closedir`, which owns the underlying directory descriptor. Do not separately close the result of `dirfd` while the stream owns it.

The null result from `readdir` has two meanings: end of directory or failure. Set `errno` to zero immediately before the call, then inspect it only if the result is null. That pattern belongs to this interface; it is not a general reason to inspect `errno` after every successful function. The [readdir manual](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/readdir.3.html) describes the distinction and the returned entry's lifetime.

Directory order is unspecified for our purposes. It may look stable during repeated runs, but the tool makes no sorting promise. The program also does not use `d_type`: applications that need reliable object metadata should obtain it through the appropriate metadata operation instead of assuming every filesystem supplies a useful type in every directory entry.

The listing skips `.` and `..`, and it does not recurse. A recursive walker needs policy for symbolic links, cycles, permission failures, directory replacement, and resource limits. Adding a recursive call without answering those questions is not a complete traversal design.

Names can contain spaces, newlines, and bytes your terminal interprets as commands. The listing therefore quotes each name and escapes bytes outside a small printable set. Non-ASCII names remain represented by their bytes, not converted into a claim about Unicode characters. This format is for inspection; it is not a shell command to execute.

## Observing a path does not reserve it

Suppose a program checks a pathname with `stat`, decides the file is acceptable, then opens that pathname. Another process can replace a directory entry between those operations. The open may reach a different object. This is a time-of-check/time-of-use race: the check and use do not act on a single stable reference.

For ordinary inspection, our tool can report observations made at different times. It does not claim to produce a transactional snapshot. For an operation that must validate the object it will actually use, opening first and applying `fstat` to the resulting descriptor is often the right starting point. Requirements about allowed paths and symlinks can require additional descriptor-relative APIs and platform-specific controls; `fstat` alone does not establish every security property.

A descriptor stabilizes the reference, not the file's contents. Another writer may still change bytes or metadata. Likewise, directory contents can change while being listed. Concurrency requires asking exactly which property must stay stable, for how long, and which operation provides that guarantee.

Consider a proposed fix: “Check the pathname twice before opening it.” Derive its effect using a schedule rather than hoping repeated checks are reassuring. At time one, the name refers to object A and the first check succeeds. At time two, the second check also sees A. At time three, another process replaces the name with one referring to B. At time four, the open obtains B. Increasing the number of checks only moves the last opportunity for replacement; it never joins the check and use into one operation.

For the narrower requirement “report metadata for the object I opened,” derive the operations from that sentence: open, then inspect the descriptor. A separate preliminary pathname check adds no guarantee of that identity. Test the distinction with a controlled pause between lookup and open, replacing the name during the pause. One forced counterexample disproves the repeated-check argument more directly than thousands of runs in which the timing happens to cooperate.

# Chapter 5 — Starting a program and collecting its result

Imagine a build helper that runs a compiler and then reports whether compilation succeeded. It receives a command and separate arguments, including filenames containing spaces. The helper must survive the compiler's execution, preserve the arguments, and distinguish an ordinary nonzero exit from termination by a signal. Its first version runs one command at a time.

Before choosing functions, identify the required separation. The requested program needs an execution context. The helper needs its own continuing context to observe the result. Replacing the helper's program would satisfy “run the compiler” but lose the observer. Our requirements therefore call for another process, or a service that provides equivalent supervision. They do not imply that every operating system must expose the same process-creation API.

## Define what the result means

Write a proposed interface before looking at the implementation:

```text
run COMMAND [ARG...]
input:   an existing vector of argument strings
output:  the child's inherited standard output and standard error
result:  the child's completion, translated into the runner's exit status
```

There are already two decisions to make. Does a command name search `PATH`, or must the caller supply a path? Does execution failure have a separate reporting channel, or share the small exit-status space with ordinary program results? Our first version searches `PATH` and uses conventional status values. Those choices make the tool convenient, while leaving some outcomes ambiguous.

Predict the result of running a nonexistent command, then running an existing program that deliberately exits with `127`. If both use the same status, what extra evidence could distinguish them? Keep that question open while deriving the lifecycle.

Before accepting “a launcher needs a child” as a rule, change one requirement. Suppose this helper only prepares arguments and hands control to a compiler; it has no later report, cleanup, or supervision to perform. What now requires the helper to survive? Nothing in that revised contract. Successful execution can replace it directly, removing a process and the helper's collection path. Execution failure still needs a defined result because replacement did not occur.

This is a useful first-principles question: **which requirement pays for each role?** In our original problem, observing and translating completion pays for the continuing parent. “Launchers usually fork” does not. Before optimizing creation or waiting, check whether the observer is required at all. If it is, retain it; if the contract changes, rederive the design. Removing necessary supervision would be a bug, while preserving unnecessary supervision would be an unexamined cost.

## Derive the minimum state

The parent needs a child identifier while it owes a collection operation, and an encoded result after collection. It does not need a copy of the child's heap or a polling counter. The important state transitions are:

```text
no child
   | successful creation
   v
child exists; parent owes collection
   | child terminates
   v
completion available; parent still owes collection
   | successful wait for this child
   v
completion collected; parent can report it
```

Termination and collection are separate events. A child can finish before its parent begins waiting. The operating system retains completion information so that scheduling order does not erase the result. Under ordinary child-status handling, an exited, uncollected child is called a zombie; it is no longer executing, but its bookkeeping remains. [Waiting for children](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/waitpid.2.html)

Our ownership invariant is: **every successfully created child has one designated collector**. Creation failure produces no such obligation. Waiting for a different child violates the assignment even if that other child also belongs to the process. A helper embedded in a larger application must coordinate collection and the application's `SIGCHLD` policy.

The progress claim is conditional: if the child eventually terminates, the parent gets execution time, and collection succeeds, the helper can finish. No waiting API can make an arbitrary child complete useful work. A timeout would be a new policy, requiring decisions about cancellation, descendants, and partial results.

## Separate contracts from our model

The interfaces supply facts that the algorithm relies on. Our surrounding assumptions and reporting policies supply different kinds of facts:

| Kind of statement | Statement used here |
| --- | --- |
| POSIX contract | `fork()` creates a child; its return values identify parent, child, or failure. Successful execution replaces the calling program image. |
| Model assumption | The launcher is single-threaded and uses ordinary child-status handling. The requested command and inherited environment are suitable for this caller. |
| Design choice | Search `PATH`, inherit standard streams, and translate signal termination to `128 + signal number`. |
| Observation | A particular child ran first, finished quickly, or printed a particular diagnostic on this machine. |

An observation about scheduling is not an ordering guarantee. An assumption about a trusted environment is not established by successful compilation. Marking these differences gives you something concrete to revisit when the launcher moves into another application.

## Choose between two viable launch designs

One design uses `fork()` to create a child, changes its setup, and calls an execution function. Another uses `posix_spawn()` or `posix_spawnp()` to describe supported setup actions and request creation and execution together. Both can preserve arguments and leave the parent available to wait.

| Design | What it makes convenient | What must be considered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Separate creation and execution | Explicit child setup and a visible lifecycle, useful for understanding descriptor redirection. | Every child failure path must terminate correctly; threaded parents impose strict restrictions. |
| `posix_spawnp` with attributes and file actions | Common launch operations expressed before creation, including argument vectors and supported descriptor actions. | The setup vocabulary is constrained; creation/setup failure and eventual child completion still need handling. |

The spawn interface is a serious implementation option, especially when its supported actions match the requirement. Our example chooses separate steps because the next chapter changes descriptor ownership between creation and execution. This is a teaching and flexibility decision, not a claim about universal performance. [The spawn interfaces](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/posix_spawn.3.html)

## Map the lifecycle onto POSIX

`fork()` returns the child's positive PID in the parent, zero in the child, and `-1` on failure. Both successful branches continue after the call. Ordinary private variables initially have equal values, but later modifications are independent. Inherited descriptors instead refer to the same underlying open file descriptions, so an inherited regular-file offset can be shared. Copying a handle and copying its resource are different operations. [POSIX `fork` specification](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/fork.3p.html)

The child calls `execvp()`. The `v` interface accepts a null-terminated argument vector; the `p` variant searches `PATH` for a command without a slash. Success replaces the program image and never returns to this code. The PID remains the child's identity. Our existing `argv` already supplies a terminating null pointer, so `&argv[1]` preserves the command's argument boundaries. [The `exec` family](https://www.man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/execlp.3.html)

The invoking shell has already handled quoting. In `build/run printf '%s\n' 'two words'`, the final argument is one string. Concatenating the vector into a command string would create a new parsing problem. `execvp()` also has a historical shell fallback for certain executable text files; the example should not be treated as a hardened launcher for untrusted executable paths.

Now attack a tempting implementation: “Call execution in the child, then let the function finish normally.” Execution returns only on failure, making that fall-through precisely the exceptional case:

```text
child attempts execution
execution fails
child falls into normal success return
parent collects exit status zero
caller concludes its requested program succeeded
```

This violates the result contract without crashing. The child must turn execution failure into an explicit failure result and stop. It uses `_exit()` rather than normal C shutdown. Otherwise, copied parent buffers and inherited exit callbacks can be acted on by the wrong process. If buffered text existed before creation and both branches flushed it, one logical message could appear twice.

## Design your branches before revealing the source

Sketch three creation outcomes and two child execution outcomes. For each arrow, identify who continues and who still owes collection. Then choose what happens if a wait is interrupted: does the child's existence change merely because the parent's wait returned early?

The reference implementation flushes parent streams before creation, saves execution's `errno` immediately, and uses a fixed child diagnostic through `write()`. That helper handles partial and interrupted writes; an unsuccessful diagnostic attempt does not justify reporting successful execution.

static _Noreturn void child_exec_failed(int code)
{
    static const char message[] = "run: exec failed\n";
    size_t done = 0;

    while (done < sizeof message - 1) {
        ssize_t n = write(STDERR_FILENO, message + done,
                          sizeof message - 1 - done);
        if (n > 0) {

            continue;
        } else {
            break;
        }
    }

        return WEXITSTATUS(status);

    if (WIFSIGNALED(status)) {

        fprintf(stderr, "run: child terminated by signal %d\n", signal_number);

The signal diagnostic supplies additional evidence for a person. A caller needing an unambiguous machine-readable termination kind needs a separate structured result, rather than interpreting the runner's exit value alone.

A diagnostic destination that blocks is another environmental dependency; this compact runner is not a bounded-time supervisor.

The single-threaded assumption also carries real weight. After a multithreaded parent forks, the child retains only the calling thread while copied memory can contain locks held by vanished threads. Before execution, it must stay within documented async-signal-safe operations. `execvp()` does not carry the same portable guarantee as `execve()` for that restricted interval. Reusing this implementation in a threaded program requires redesign, often toward spawning with supported actions. [Post-fork restrictions](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/fork.2.html)

# Chapter 6 — Pipes and the ownership of an ending

Suppose one existing program produces bytes and another summarizes them. You want the summary to begin while production continues, and the amount of intermediate data might exceed your available memory. You also need the final result of both programs. The parent should supervise the connection without copying every byte itself.

These constraints suggest a bounded communication channel and concurrent execution. They do not yet specify a pipe, a buffer size, or an exit-status convention. Begin by separating the data path from the supervision path: bytes flow between commands, while completion information returns to their parent.

Our interface will be:

```sh
build/pipeline printf 'hello\n' -- wc -c
```

Before running it, predict three things: how many bytes enter the consumer, what event lets the consumer stop reading, and whether a successful consumer proves that its producer succeeded. The first question is arithmetic. The other two determine most of the design.

## Compare ways to connect the programs

A temporary file is a viable intermediate representation. Run the producer, check its result, then give the completed file to the consumer. The file can be inspected or replayed, and the commands need not run together. This costs storage proportional to the intermediate output and delays consumption until the selected completion boundary. Creation, permissions, removal, and storage exhaustion become part of the design.

A pipe instead permits both programs to run concurrently with a bounded kernel buffer. A slow consumer constrains its producer through backpressure. The intermediate bytes are not retained for replay. A hang can arise from relationships among processes and open references, even when every individual call behaves correctly.

| Requirement | Temporary file | Direct pipe |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Consumer starts before producer finishes | Requires an additional concurrent-file protocol. | Fits ordinary streaming use. |
| Inspect or replay the complete intermediate result | Natural after a successful producer. | Requires additional storage or duplication. |
| Bound intermediate buffering independently of total output | Needs a different storage policy or a size limit. | Finite pipe capacity provides backpressure. |
| Explain completion | Producer status and selected file-completion policy. | Both child statuses plus precise stream-closure ownership. |

We choose the pipe because early consumption and bounded buffering matter for this experiment. If replay became the primary requirement, the file design would deserve reconsideration. Neither choice eliminates the need to define what partial output means after producer failure.

## Derive the state that matters

For reasoning, model the channel with three quantities: buffered bytes `B`, open write references `W`, and open read references `R`. The kernel maintains the actual channel state; our program need not duplicate these counters. The parent needs only its owned endpoints, the child PIDs it must collect, and their results.

For a positive-length blocking read, the important cases are: available bytes can be consumed; an empty pipe with possible writers requires waiting; an empty pipe with no writers produces EOF. Thus “the producer finished” is insufficient. A different process may still hold a write reference. [POSIX read rules for pipes](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/read.3p.html)

The intended final topology is:

```text
producer stdout ---> [ bounded byte buffer ] ---> consumer stdin

parent:          owns no channel endpoints; owns collection obligations
producer:        owns its output reference; no unused channel references
consumer:        owns its input reference; no unused channel references
```

This gives two invariants. Bytes enter through the intended producer output and leave through the intended consumer input. Every additional reference has a defined removal point before the parent waits for completion. The first concerns correct data flow; the second is also essential for progress.

Consider a proposed version with two pipes: the parent reads the producer's bytes into a buffer and writes them to the consumer. Before asking how large that buffer should be, ask what required result the relay creates. If the parent neither transforms nor inspects the bytes, the direct connection already satisfies our contract. Deleting the relay removes its buffer, its partially forwarded range, and its transfer-failure paths. We can eliminate work before trying to make that work faster.

Now add a genuine requirement: the parent must retain a copy of every intermediate byte for later inspection. The direct connection no longer suffices by itself. Forwarding with storage, or another duplication design, may earn its added state. Ask what “later inspection” means before implementing it: inspecting child statuses requires supervision, but inspecting their bytes requires a data-path change. Similar wording can hide very different obligations.

The external interface supplies ordering and EOF rules. Our model assumes ordinary blocking descriptors, commands that use their streams as intended, and no requirement to retain the intermediate output. Choosing two commands, a `--` separator, and a particular result policy are application decisions. Actual scheduling and pipe capacity are observations, not assumptions the algorithm should depend on.

## Find the deadlock before writing the happy path

Consider a sequential plan: create the producer, wait for it, then create the consumer. A short output may make this appear successful. A larger output produces a counterexample:

```text
producer fills the pipe
producer waits for a reader to remove bytes
parent waits for producer termination
consumer has not been created
```

The wait graph is closed: the action that could release the writer depends on that writer finishing. Increasing capacity merely moves the failing input size. Starting both commands before waiting changes the dependency graph and removes this cycle. Pipe capacity is finite and not a portable fixed constant. [Pipe capacity and blocking](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/pipe.7.html)

Now consider a different plan: both commands run, but the parent retains its write endpoint “for cleanup at the end.” After the producer exits and the consumer drains buffered data:

```text
consumer waits for EOF: parent's write reference still exists
parent waits for consumer: plans to close its reference afterward
```

No byte is corrupted, and no invalid pointer is involved. This is a liveness failure caused by an ownership decision. Closing the parent's reference before waiting removes the cycle. Sleeping, changing priority, or closing only the producer's descriptor cannot repair the missing ownership transition.

## Map the topology onto descriptors

`pipe(ends)` creates the channel and returns its read descriptor in `ends[0]` and write descriptor in `ends[1]`. Forking copies references into each child. A possible scheduling point after both forks, before child cleanup, has both original endpoints in all three processes. Other schedules perform some cleanup earlier; correctness must survive either order. [POSIX pipe creation](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/pipe.3p.html)

The producer needs its standard output to refer to the write end. `dup2(ends[1], STDOUT_FILENO)` makes that relationship; the consumer similarly redirects standard input. The original pipe descriptors still exist afterward. Duplication creates another reference rather than consuming the source.

`dup2()` replaces the target descriptor as one operation. Separately closing a target and calling `dup()` opens a gap in which another allocation could take the desired number. When its source and target differ, successful `dup2()` clears the target's close-on-exec flag, so the redirected standard stream survives execution. [POSIX `dup2` specification](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/dup2.3p.html)

The example checks that descriptors 0, 1, and 2 are initially open. Its new pipe endpoints are consequently above 2, so closing the originals cannot close the newly redirected standard stream. This is a checked precondition, not a universal fact about pipe descriptor numbers. A more general launcher can move temporary descriptors above the standard range and handle closed standard streams deliberately.

## Review exceptional transitions

The original endpoints are marked `FD_CLOEXEC` and explicitly closed in each child. Marking expresses their intended lifetime; explicit closure establishes the topology before execution. The gap between `pipe()` and setting these flags is acceptable under this example's single-threaded launch model. A threaded launcher must reconsider concurrent descriptor creation and execution.

Each child redirects, closes the original ends, executes, or terminates through `_exit()`. As in Chapter 5, the `execvp()` design assumes a single-threaded launcher. The first `--` splits two existing argument vectors; the invoking shell handles quoting. A literal `--` cannot be passed to the left command with this grammar. A library interface taking two separate vectors would avoid that restriction.

If the second fork fails, a first child already exists. The parent closes its endpoints, terminates that owned child, and collects it. If either parent close fails after both children exist, it terminates both before collection: an uncertain retained endpoint could otherwise prevent EOF. This avoids blindly retrying a descriptor number and avoids waiting for a closure condition the parent may itself be preventing. See Chapter 3 for interrupted-close portability.

The cancellation policy uses `SIGKILL` for an incomplete setup. That is an explicit policy for this small tool, not orderly application shutdown. It addresses the immediate children, not every descendant an arbitrary command might create. Commands with other blocking dependencies can still wait indefinitely; this example does not provide general job control, signal forwarding, or time bounds.

## Decide what success reports

A byte stream does not carry the producer's process status. A consumer can successfully count an empty stream produced by a failing program. Returning only the right command's status is one useful policy for shell composition; checking both statuses is another. Our tool returns a nonzero right status if present, otherwise the left status, making producer failure visible.

There is a further consequence: a consumer may intentionally stop early. Once all read references are closed, a later write raises `SIGPIPE`; when that signal is ignored, the write reports `EPIPE`. Our tool can therefore report a nonzero producer result for an intentional early-consumer exit. A specialized pipeline might classify that outcome differently. [POSIX write errors](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/write.3p.html)

Also distinguish a byte stream from messages. Two writes do not require two corresponding reads. Atomic insertion relative to competing writers does not establish read boundaries. A record-oriented protocol would need lengths, delimiters, or another framing rule. Counting bytes needs no such rule, which is one reason it makes a useful first consumer.

# Chapter 7 — Receiving a request to stop

Imagine a small service that owns an output file. A controller must be able to ask it to stop, let it finish its cleanup, and collect its result. The request may arrive while the service is between ordinary instructions. We want a protocol that preserves the request until the service can act on it, without forcing cleanup to run inside an arbitrary interrupted operation.

For the first experiment, remove useful work and the output file. Keep only startup, a stop request, and normal termination. Simplifying the payload lets us investigate the synchronization problem directly. The eventual service can add resources after this ordering is understood.

The requirement is an orderly response to a supported request after announced readiness. It is not a promise to recover from every possible process termination. Establishing that boundary is part of the design, before choosing a signal number or handler.

## Derive the protocol before the mechanism

The service passes through these logical states:

```text
initializing --> ready --> stop accepted --> cleaning up --> finished
                   ^
          controller may send now
```

The controller needs evidence that the receiver has installed its policy. The receiver needs the request to remain observable if it arrives just before waiting. Because stopping is an idempotent decision, a boolean “stop requested” is enough information; ten stop requests need not produce ten separate cleanups.

This gives a safety property: cleanup executes once through ordinary controlled flow, with resources still valid. It also gives a conditional progress property: after a supported request and readiness, the receiver eventually reaches cleanup if it gets execution time and its remaining operations complete. Whether cleanup itself can block indefinitely is a separate design question.

Predict the dangerous intervals. What happens if the controller sends before readiness? What if it sends after readiness but just before the receiver begins waiting? A correct protocol must either handle each interval or exclude it explicitly through a justified precondition. “The controller probably waits long enough” supplies neither.

## Distinguish the operating-system rules

POSIX signals provide notifications with several separate controls. A signal's **disposition** specifies its delivery action: default behavior, ignoring, or a handler. A thread's **mask** specifies blocked signals. A generated blocked signal can remain **pending** until it is accepted or unblocked. Blocking therefore preserves a different policy from ignoring. [Signal concepts](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/signal.7.html)

These are supplied interface rules. We cannot derive their exact shape from processor hardware, and we should not substitute an intuitive meaning for a specified one. In particular, ordinary signals can coalesce: several occurrences of one signal need not become several independently countable events. That fits our idempotent stop request but would fail a requirement to count every submitted job.

Our chosen notifications are `SIGINT` and `SIGTERM`. Their symbolic names express the source-level interface; raw numeric values should not become a portable protocol. `SIGKILL` and `SIGSTOP` cannot be caught, blocked, or ignored. Consequently, the service's cleanup guarantee must be conditional on the supported termination path. [Signal dispositions and restrictions](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/signal.7.html)

Separate those rules from the model around them:

| Kind of statement | Statement used here |
| --- | --- |
| External contract | Blocked signals in the waited-for set can be accepted through `sigwait`; ordinary signals need not count every occurrence. |
| Model assumption | One thread controls this policy, and the controller waits for readiness before sending. |
| Design choice | Either supported request triggers one orderly exit with success status. |
| Observation | A particular PID was printed and one test completed after a particular delay. |

The observed delay cannot establish a deadline guarantee. If shutdown must finish within a fixed interval, that requirement reaches every blocking operation in the cleanup path.

Work backward from a proposed requirement: “exit within five seconds, after uploading final metrics and saving all pending work.” Suppose the upload must await a remote reply, with no bound on when that reply arrives. Those requirements cannot jointly supply the deadline. Faster signal handling cannot bound an unbounded dependency after acceptance.

First ask which results are essential. If the metrics upload is optional, remove it from the required shutdown path or give it an explicit abandonment policy. If saving pending work is mandatory, its duration still needs a bound or the deadline needs revision; a timeout cannot manufacture saved data. Only then choose a waiting mechanism. To investigate an uncertain dependency, make the metrics receiver withhold its reply and observe which shutdown state stalls. That experiment can disprove a claimed deadline for that path. One prompt reply cannot prove the deadline for all runs.

## Compare two viable ways to receive the event

One design installs a minimal handler that records a flag. Ordinary code inspects the flag and performs cleanup. Waiting must coordinate the flag check with signal delivery; otherwise it can miss the transition that should wake it.

A second design blocks the selected signals and accepts one synchronously with `sigwait()`. After the function returns, normal code can process the result. For this program, whose only job is to await shutdown, that design needs no user-maintained request flag: the operating system's pending state and the program's control flow provide the necessary state.

| Design | Main benefit | Obligation it introduces |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minimal handler plus a coordinated wait | Can record an event while ordinary code runs between defined checkpoints. | Keep the handler restricted, and make testing and sleeping agree on the signal mask. |
| Blocked set plus `sigwait` | Turns acceptance into an ordinary function result with ordinary cleanup afterward. | Keep waited-for signals blocked and allocate an execution path that can wait for them. |

A handler-based design is viable; its correctness depends on more than choosing a special flag type. A dedicated waiting thread is another useful form of the second design when work must continue concurrently. Chapter 9 provides the ownership and synchronization concepts needed for that extension.

## Break the intuitive handler loop

Suppose a handler sets a flag and the main program repeatedly checks the flag, calling `pause()` when no request appears. The counterexample needs only three events:

```text
main observes stop_requested == 0
handler runs and sets stop_requested = 1
main calls pause and waits for another signal
```

The notification was delivered, the flag holds the desired value, and the program can still wait forever. This is a lost-wakeup problem, not a compiler optimization problem. Making the flag volatile or adding a delay does not make the check and wait atomic.

A handler-based repair blocks the signal while inspecting state, then uses `sigsuspend()` to change the mask and wait as a coordinated operation. The surrounding loop rechecks state when control returns. The temporary mask change closes the gap in which the notification could be consumed before the intended wait. [The `sigsuspend` interface](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/sigsuspend.2.html)

There is also a safety failure in “just clean up inside the handler.” A handler might interrupt `printf()` while the library is updating internal stream state, then call `printf()` again on that inconsistent state. It might attempt to acquire a lock already held by the interrupted execution. POSIX specifies a restricted async-signal-safe set precisely because ordinary operations cannot all tolerate that interruption context. [Async-signal safety](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man7/signal-safety.7.html)

A minimal illustrative handler can assign a `volatile sig_atomic_t` flag. That does not make a larger data structure safe and does not replace thread synchronization. If a handler calls an allowed operation that changes `errno`, it must preserve the interrupted code's value. The simplest safe handler remains part of a larger synchronization design.

## Choose the synchronous design and prove its ordering

Our receiver first blocks `SIGINT` and `SIGTERM`, establishes dispositions, and then prints and flushes its readiness line. Finally it calls `sigwait()`. Consider the relevant arrival intervals:

| Request arrives | Consequence under this protocol |
| --- | --- |
| Before readiness | Outside the cooperating controller's contract; startup protection is not yet established at every earlier instant. |
| After readiness, before the wait | It can remain pending for acceptance. |
| While waiting | Acceptance lets the call return with the selected signal. |
| During cleanup | Selected signals remain blocked; this one-shot design does not start cleanup again. |

The key invariant is that the selected signals remain blocked throughout the announced interval. Readiness is therefore evidence of a completed state transition, not a statement about elapsed startup time. A sleep in the controller could only estimate that transition.

`sigwait()` returns zero on success and places the signal number in its output parameter. Its error result is an error number directly, rather than a `-1` result whose explanation must be read from `errno`. Handling it like `read()` would mix two contracts. [POSIX `sigwait` specification](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/sigwait.3p.html)

Before opening the reference implementation, write the three operations “block, announce, accept” and justify each ordering edge. Include the flush in your explanation: output buffered only in the receiver's memory cannot yet serve as evidence for another process.

        sigaction(SIGTERM, &action, NULL) == -1) {
        perror("sigaction");
        return 1;
    }
    if (printf("ready pid=%jd\n", (intmax_t)getpid()) < 0 ||
        fflush(stdout) == EOF) {
        perror("stdout");
        return 1;
    }

    int received;
    int error = sigwait(&signals, &received);
    if (error != 0) {
        fprintf(stderr, "sigwait: %s\n", strerror(error));
        return 1;
    }
    const char *name = received == SIGINT ? "SIGINT" : "SIGTERM";
    if (printf("received %s; cleanup complete\n", name) < 0 ||
        fflush(stdout) == EOF) {
        perror("stdout");
        return 1;
    }
    /* Leave these signals blocked while main returns and stdio shuts down. */
    return 0;
}
```


</details>

The program restores default dispositions while the signals are blocked. A background shell command can inherit an ignored `SIGINT`; resetting that disposition avoids depending on inherited ignore behavior. This does not deliver a blocked signal immediately. Disposition and mask are separate controls. [POSIX signal actions](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/sigaction.3p.html)

After acceptance, `printf()` is ordinary program work, not a handler call. The example has no application resource to release, so its cleanup consists of finishing the normal path and stream handling. A real service would fill this position with an explicit resource order: stop accepting work, settle owned work, then release resources still needed by that work. Reporting “cleanup complete” would not itself prove the process has exited; a supervising parent still collects completion.

## Change the assumptions carefully

The example is single-threaded and uses `sigprocmask()`. In a threaded application, use `pthread_sigmask()` and establish the policy before creating workers, which inherit the mask. A process-directed signal can otherwise reach an eligible thread that does not block it. Dispositions remain process-wide even though masks are per-thread. [POSIX mask rules](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/sigprocmask.3p.html)

The waiting program also does no simultaneous blocking input. Adding an indefinite `read()` before `sigwait()` could prevent the chosen acceptance path from running. A dedicated waiting thread, or an event-loop wakeup design, addresses that new progress requirement. Adding unsafe handler work does not remove it. Chapter 11 develops the event-loop side.

Finally, inherited masks can survive execution. A launcher that changes its own policy must decide what the new program should inherit. A program that appears unresponsive to an interrupt may be following an inherited blocked mask rather than an installed ignore disposition.

# Chapter 8 — Reading a file through virtual memory

Suppose you must count occurrences of one byte in a binary file. The file may contain zero bytes and may be empty. You need an exact count, bounded index arithmetic, and a clear failure result. Begin with an ordinary, stable regular file whose size fits the process's address-space model. Those conditions are input assumptions to examine, not properties established merely by opening a path.

The counting problem does not require a memory mapping. A buffered read loop could solve it. We will compare that design with viewing the file through virtual memory, then choose a mapping so that its different ownership and failure boundaries become visible.

Before choosing, predict whether a successful setup call proves that every subsequent access will be fast and valid. What could another process change after the setup call returns? Those questions matter more than whether the scan is written with an array subscript or a function call.

## Derive the counting algorithm independently of I/O

Let `L` be the number of bytes to inspect, `wanted` the byte value, `i` the next position, and `count` the occurrences already seen. A useful loop invariant is:

```text
0 <= count <= i <= L
count equals the number of wanted bytes in positions [0, i)
```

Initially `i` and `count` are zero. If the next byte matches, increase `count`; then advance `i`. After processing exactly `L` bytes, the invariant provides the answer. The empty input already satisfies the completed condition and therefore has count zero.

This reasoning exposes two requirements that the storage interface must satisfy. Every accessed position must belong to the permitted range, and the bytes being inspected must have the stability required by “exact count.” A loop can stay within its numeric bounds while reading a mixture of versions. Bounds safety and snapshot consistency are different properties.

Can we remove most of the scan before making it faster? For arbitrary bytes, an exact answer, and no trusted summary, consider any algorithm that leaves one position unexamined. Construct two same-length inputs identical at every examined position: the unexamined byte matches `wanted` in one and does not in the other. The algorithm has the same evidence for both, but their correct counts differ. It therefore cannot guarantee the answer. In the worst case, the input contains information we must inspect throughout its length, even if one machine instruction examines several bytes.

This lower bound belongs to the problem's assumptions. A trusted count associated with the exact immutable version could eliminate a later scan; accepting an estimate could permit sampling. Both change the available evidence or the required answer. Merely choosing `mmap()` changes neither. Establish what work is necessary before arguing about how cheaply an interface performs it.

C strings would be the wrong representation for arbitrary binary input: an embedded zero is data, not an ending marker. An explicit length and `unsigned char` access preserve all byte values. The command accepts the desired value as decimal `0` through `255`; an argument itself cannot carry an embedded null byte.

## Compare ways to supply the bytes

A streaming design owns a fixed buffer and repeatedly calls `read()`. Its invariant extends the count across successfully received chunks. It can process a pipe as well as a regular file, and transfer failures appear at explicit call boundaries. Total input need not occupy one contiguous virtual range.

A mapped design asks the operating system to associate a file range with virtual addresses. The loop can access that range directly, and repeated random access may be convenient. Mapping does not establish that every page is resident or that later file changes are harmless. The operating system may perform work when the program first touches an address.

| Design | Useful consequence | Cost or obligation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Buffered `read` | Bounded explicit buffer; works with streams; familiar partial-I/O handling. | Manage chunk boundaries and transfer progress. Concurrent mutation still needs a consistency policy. |
| Whole-file mapping | Direct indexing; a compact scan and convenient reuse of the same range. | Require a mappable object and enough contiguous virtual space; manage mapping lifetime and backing-file changes. |
| Positioned `pread` calls | Fetch selected file ranges without changing the descriptor's shared offset. | Requires an appropriate seekable object, offset arithmetic, and partial-transfer handling. |

`pread()` supplies the positioned-read contract; it does not supply a file snapshot or solve concurrent changes by itself. [Positioned I/O](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/pread.2.html)

Our one-pass byte counter has no requirement that makes mapping inevitable. The educational choice lets us explore another interface using an already understood algorithm. A performance decision would need measurements of a specified workload, including access pattern and cache state. Counting fewer explicit `read()` calls is not a measurement of all work performed.

## Specify the assumptions around a mapping

Virtual addresses identify locations in a process's address space. The operating system manages mappings at page granularity, but page size is not a universal constant such as 4096. If an algorithm needs it, query `sysconf(_SC_PAGESIZE)` and check the result. Our whole-file mapping starts at offset zero and lets the system select the virtual address, avoiding alignment arithmetic in this first version.

The roles of the statements in our design are different:

| Kind of statement | Statement used here |
| --- | --- |
| External contract | `mmap` creates an address-space mapping; zero length is invalid; its failure sentinel is `MAP_FAILED`. |
| Model assumption | The ordinary regular file remains stable throughout inspection and the selected range is usable. |
| Design choice | Use a read-only private mapping, reject excessive sizes, and special-case an empty file. |
| Observation | One run completed without a fault and had a measured duration on one machine. |

A positive observation does not validate the stability assumption for another producer. POSIX explicitly leaves effects of accesses into portions added or removed after mapping unspecified. The guarantee we need must therefore come from the application's file-lifecycle policy. [POSIX memory mappings](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/mmap.3p.html)

## Derive ownership from two independent lifetimes

Opening a file gives us a descriptor. Mapping it creates another kind of resource whose lifetime is separate:

```text
file descriptor: open ----------- close
mapping:                 mmap ---------------------- munmap
borrowed byte pointer:        usable during the mapping's valid lifetime
numeric count:                    accumulated -------- usable afterward
```

After successful mapping, closing the descriptor does not end the mapping. Conversely, closing the descriptor alone is not how we release the address range. `munmap()` ends that lifetime, after which pointers into it are unusable. [Mapping and descriptor lifetime](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/mmap.2.html)

The implementation therefore needs to retain the mapping's base address and length until release. Its scan pointer borrows access; it owns no independent lifetime. The final numeric count does not borrow the mapped bytes, so it can be printed after unmapping.

Derive the failure cleanup from acquired resources rather than from a generic label called “error.” Before mapping, only the descriptor is owned. After mapping and before closing, both are owned. During scanning, the mapping remains. A failed descriptor close after successful mapping still leaves a mapping to release. Drawing those states makes missed cleanup visible before the code grows more branches.

## Validate the boundary between integer domains

`fstat()` reports size in signed `off_t`; the mapping interface accepts a `size_t` length. Casting does not prove representability. Reject a negative size and check the positive value against the destination range before converting. Otherwise, the numeric value used for bounds could cease to mean the measured file length.

Our example also restricts length to `PTRDIFF_MAX`. This is an explicit whole-file design limit that keeps the region within the range representable by pointer differences. It is not a promise that every smaller mapping will succeed. Available virtual address space and other resource limits still matter.

For length zero, return the mathematical answer after closing the descriptor, without requesting a zero-length mapping. This adapts the operating-system contract to the application's broader input domain. Rejecting an empty file simply because one chosen mechanism cannot map it would confuse a mechanism restriction with the counting problem.

Before revealing the source, write the scan invariant and the three resource states. Predict the cleanup needed if mapping fails, if closing fails afterward, and if final output fails. Then choose whether a zero byte ends the scan and justify your answer from the input contract.

## Challenge the assumption that private means frozen

“Private mapping” sounds like a private snapshot, but that intuition supplies a guarantee the interface does not make. The flag describes how writes through a private mapping relate to the underlying object; it does not promise a frozen version immune to external mutation. A shared mapping has different sharing behavior, but it likewise does not add an application transaction or locking protocol. [Mapping flags](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/mmap.2.html)

A counterexample to “we checked the size, so reads are safe” is:

```text
reader observes positive file size
reader successfully maps that range
writer truncates the same file to zero
reader touches an address whose backing has disappeared
```

On the target systems, such access can produce `SIGBUS` rather than return an ordinary I/O error to the loop. Checking the size again only moves the interval in which truncation can occur. It does not prevent the writer's action after the second check. [Mapped-file access beyond the object](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/mmap.2.html)

The missing guarantee can be supplied in several ways. A producer can publish completed immutable file objects and leave existing objects unchanged. Cooperating readers and writers can use a locking protocol around the relevant lifetime. A reader can work on an independently produced stable copy, provided the copy operation itself has a consistency rule. These choices change producer behavior or establish coordination; switching to a different load syntax cannot create that agreement.

Ordinary reads may make transfer failures easier to handle, but a sequence of successful reads can still combine versions of a concurrently modified file. Avoiding a mapping fault and obtaining a coherent snapshot are separate requirements. Also separate sharing, synchronization, and durability: writable `MAP_SHARED` access does not by itself ensure that an application update is atomic or survives a crash. That would require a storage protocol beyond this counter.

# Chapter 9 — Deriving a shared-state protocol

Four workers each complete 25,000 units of work. We want an exact final total, and we want every completed increment to be represented in one common counter while the workers run. Storage must stay bounded. The owner of that counter must survive every worker that can access it. If starting a worker fails, the program must finish dealing with the workers already started and report failure.

These requirements come before threads or locks. They describe an accounting problem with concurrent contributors. They also expose a distinction: obtaining a final sum and maintaining a live shared total are different requirements. Keep both visible while evaluating designs.

## Separate a requirement from a mechanism

Our **application contract** requires exactly 100,000 increments on success. Our **model** is that workers may be delayed between any operations; source-code order in one worker does not schedule another. The **platform contract** supplies shared memory, thread creation, and synchronization operations. Our **design choice** is to use a mutex for the common counter. An observed total of 100,000 on your machine is one execution that agrees with the contract. It is not the reason the design is correct.

Three plausible implementations deserve consideration. One execution path can do all the work, avoiding coordination. Separate worker counters can be combined after the workers finish, avoiding competing writes. A shared counter can be protected during each update, providing the live common state we requested. The final-only variant would often favor private counters; this chapter intentionally retains the live-total requirement to study a shared invariant. None of these architectures acquires a speed advantage merely from its name.

Before optimizing that shared update, find the decision that needs it. Suppose “live total” came from a dashboard that refreshes once a second. That refresh interval alone does not tell us whether the displayed count may lag, must be exact at each refresh, or must account for an operation before its completion is acknowledged. Those are different contracts, even if their screenshots look alike.

If the owner accepts delayed progress reports and an exact final result, workers can accumulate contributions privately and publish batches through a synchronized protocol. This removes some shared updates but introduces unpublished work and a rule for publishing the final partial batch. If acknowledgement requires the contribution to be committed first, batching cannot preserve immediate acknowledgement without further changes. Write the permitted delay before selecting a batch size. The first-principles step is to challenge why each shared update exists; deleting it is valid only after the resulting behavior satisfies the agreed requirement. This chapter keeps the original per-increment contract.

Threads share a process's globals, heap, and descriptors, while having separate stacks and execution state. That platform arrangement makes a shared pointer possible. It does not answer when the pointed-to object may be read or destroyed. Those are obligations of our design.

The repair must therefore establish a valid ordering, not merely make the illustrated schedule less likely. `volatile` does not make an increment atomic or synchronize threads. Adding a sleep changes timing without creating the necessary relationship. Running a million successful trials does not add an ordering edge either.

We need an indivisible state transition relative to the other workers. A mutex is a supplied mechanism for exclusive access and memory synchronization. All accesses that participate in the shared-counter protocol must follow the same rule: hold this mutex while workers can modify the counter. A hardware intuition about cache coherence cannot replace the C and pthread contracts.

## Derive the state and the invariant

Before creating workers, we need a counter initialized to zero, an initialized mutex, and storage for thread identifiers. During creation, we also need the number of successful creations. A bare array of identifiers cannot tell us which entries are valid after a partial failure.

Our main invariant is: **at every release of the counter mutex, the counter equals the number of increments committed so far**. Initially both are zero. A worker acquires the mutex, adds one, and releases it. No other participating worker can observe or modify the protected state in the middle of that transition. Induction over completed critical sections gives the accounting argument.

Two lifetime invariants accompany it: the shared object remains alive while any started worker may use it, and the normal and handled creation-failure paths join every successfully created joinable worker exactly once before destroying shared synchronization state. Unexpected synchronization failures follow a separate fatal-process policy in this example. A correct arithmetic invariant cannot rescue a dangling pointer.

A queue makes the distinction sharper. Its head, tail, occupancy, and elements jointly describe one state. Locking four assignments separately can expose a combination that never represented a valid queue. Protect the relationship across the whole transition. A lock is associated with an invariant; its job is not merely to decorate suspicious source lines.

The common counter may be read without its mutex after every worker has been joined successfully. That exception follows from the lifecycle: no worker can still modify it, and joining supplies the required synchronization. It is not a prediction that the workers are probably finished.

        check_thread(pthread_mutex_lock(&shared->mutex), "mutex lock");
        ++shared->count;
        check_thread(pthread_mutex_unlock(&shared->mutex), "mutex unlock");
    }
    return NULL;

    check_thread(pthread_mutex_destroy(&shared.mutex), "mutex destroy");
    if (create_error != 0) {
        fprintf(stderr, "create: %s\n", strerror(create_error));
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    const unsigned long expected = (unsigned long)WORKERS * ITERATIONS;
    if (printf("count=%lu expected=%lu\n", shared.count, expected) < 0 ||
        fflush(stdout) == EOF) {
        perror("stdout");
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }

}

## Correct state does not guarantee progress

The invariant proves a safety property: increments are not lost or duplicated by competing protected updates. Progress requires more. Workers must receive execution time, critical sections must finish, and the program must avoid cycles of waiting. These assumptions are not consequences of the final count.

Consider two workers and two locks. A holds the first and waits for the second; B holds the second and waits for the first. Neither corrupts the protected data. Neither can finish. Establishing one lock acquisition order removes this particular cycle. It does not promise a wall-clock completion bound under arbitrary scheduling.

Likewise, holding the counter lock while performing a blocking network read preserves exclusion but can prevent every other contributor from progressing. Short critical sections are valuable because they reduce the work that others must wait behind. First identify the complete state transition; then move independent work outside it without weakening the invariant.

The sample makes no speedup claim. A useful hypothesis would be: “For independent arithmetic work of this size, private results reduce coordination cost compared with a lock per update.” To test it, keep the work, compiler, worker count, and result checks comparable. Chapter 12 explains why a compiler may simplify a private counting loop dramatically. An observed improvement must be attributed to the generated work actually measured.

## Derive waiting from a queue predicate

Now change the problem: a consumer must wait until a queue contains an item or has been permanently closed. Busy checking wastes execution time while nothing changes. A fixed sleep adds delay and still supplies no state synchronization. We want to suspend until progress may be possible.

A naive plan checks `queue.count == 0`, unlocks, then sleeps awaiting a notification. Predict the failure if the producer inserts and notifies between that unlock and the start of the wait. Another naive plan keeps the mutex locked while sleeping. Predict why the producer cannot repair the empty queue in that version.

The required operation must connect releasing the mutex with beginning the wait, preventing that gap relative to the producer's protected update. POSIX condition waiting provides this operation and reacquires the mutex before a successful return. A wakeup does not guarantee the predicate is true: another consumer may have taken the item, and spurious wakeups are permitted. [Condition-wait contract](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/pthread_cond_wait.3p.html)

That contract motivates the loop below. It is structural pseudocode in C syntax; a complete implementation must check errors and initialize all objects.

```c
pthread_mutex_lock(&queue.mutex);
while (queue.count == 0 && !queue.closed)
    pthread_cond_wait(&queue.changed, &queue.mutex);
/* While holding the mutex: remove an item, or recognize closed-and-empty. */
pthread_mutex_unlock(&queue.mutex);
```

The queue state remembers whether work exists. The notification merely tells sleepers to reconsider. A producer changes state under the same mutex and signals. Closing sets `closed` under the mutex and broadcasts so every waiting consumer can discover that no more work will arrive. Without a closing state, an empty queue cannot distinguish “wait for a future item” from “there will never be another item.”

This is also why cooperative shutdown is attractive: workers leave through ordinary control flow and release owned resources. Thread cancellation introduces additional questions about the locks and objects owned at the interruption point.

# Chapter 10 — Deriving a protocol from incomplete information

We want two local processes to exchange a request and a verified reply. The request may contain arbitrary bytes, has a maximum size of 4096 bytes, and must be returned unchanged. The receiver must reject an incomplete request rather than silently accepting a prefix. Neither side should allocate memory proportional to an unchecked value supplied by the other.

These are application requirements. They do not yet prescribe TCP, a header, or a C function. Our chosen experiment uses one TCP connection on IPv4 loopback, handles at most one request, and exits. The command-line client accepts a string argument, so it cannot originate embedded NUL bytes even though the wire format can represent them.

## What information is missing?

The relevant transport contract is an ordered byte stream. TCP does not preserve the boundaries of application writes. One write can require several reads; several writes can be returned together. A positive receive count tells us how many bytes arrived in that call. An orderly peer shutdown eventually yields a zero count after available data is consumed. [Socket interface](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/socket.2.html), [Receive behavior](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/recv.2.html)

Imagine a sender writes five bytes spelling `hello`. Before continuing, predict what a receiver that performs exactly one read into a 4096-byte array can conclude if the call returns two. Has the sender violated the protocol? Has a complete request arrived? What information would distinguish a short complete message from a prefix of a longer one?

<details>
<summary>Review the missing information</summary>


The transport has not violated its contract. The read can return `he` while `llo` arrives later. Two bytes alone cannot distinguish a complete two-byte message from an incomplete five-byte message. The receiver needs an application rule for completion. Increasing its buffer cannot supply that missing rule.


</details>

This is a reasoning pattern worth preserving: identify what the observer can know, then add exactly the information required by the application. An observed five-byte read during a local test does not strengthen the transport contract.

## Compare ways to identify completion

A delimiter, a declared length, and connection termination are plausible designs. Delimiters are convenient for text, but arbitrary bytes require escaping or a restricted alphabet. Termination can delimit one request if the client closes its sending direction and leaves the receiving direction available for the reply; it couples message completion to connection lifecycle. A declared length supports arbitrary bytes and can later support several frames on a connection, but introduces a size field that must be validated.

We choose a length prefix. That choice follows our binary-message and explicit-validation goals. The exact four-byte width and byte-order convention remain design choices; they are not mathematical consequences of networking.

| Field | Encoding | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Length | Four-byte unsigned integer, network byte order | Between zero and 4096 inclusive |
| Body | Exactly the declared number of bytes | No terminator or escaping |

The length excludes the header. An empty body is therefore represented by a complete four-byte header containing zero. It is different from a connection that sends no bytes at all.

Challenge the inherited width before treating it as necessary. How many distinct lengths must a fixed-width length field represent? Could one byte work? Could two? Commit your answer from the allowed values, without using a familiar protocol as the justification.

<details>
<summary>Derive the information needed</summary>


Lengths zero through 4096 require 4097 distinct values. Twelve bits represent only 4096 values, so this field needs at least thirteen bits; two whole bytes suffice. Our four-byte field is a convenient convention, not a lower bound. Reducing it would save two bytes per frame while retaining length validation and partial-header state. Whether that saving matters depends on the workload. If every body were required to contain exactly 4096 bytes, a revised protocol could omit the length field entirely because the agreement already supplies that information. This would remove variable-length messages from the contract, so it requires a real change in need. Derive the information first, then choose its representation.


</details>

Network byte order puts the most significant byte first. We use `htonl` to encode and `ntohl` to decode the fixed-width header. Sending an ordinary host integer's bytes without a specified encoding would depend on representation; sending an entire C structure would add layout and padding dependencies. The required property is agreement about the bytes, while these conversion functions implement our selected convention. [Byte-order conversions](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/byteorder.3.html)

## Derive the receiver's memory and states

Before the first byte, the receiver needs four bytes of header storage and a count of how much is present. It cannot interpret an incomplete header. After collecting four bytes, it needs a validated body length and a count of body bytes received. Only after that count reaches the declared length is the request complete.

The invariants are small enough to state directly: header bytes collected stay between zero and four; a validated length stays at most 4096; body bytes collected stay between zero and that length. Buffer addresses always refer to the unfilled suffix, and each positive transfer advances progress exactly once. The maximum is checked before reading the body, so even an enormous declared length cannot expand our storage requirement.

For blocking I/O, these states can live in a function's local variables and loop position. Chapter 11 will move them into longer-lived objects when a handler must return before completion. The logical state exists in either design, whether or not a `struct` names it.

Now classify EOF. Commit answers for four cases before opening the review: no header bytes, two header bytes, a complete header announcing five followed by two body bytes, and a complete zero-length header.

<details>
<summary>Review the EOF cases</summary>


With no header bytes, no request started; our server chooses to close successfully without replying. Two header bytes mean a truncated header. Five promised body bytes followed by only two mean a truncated body. A zero-length header describes a complete request immediately and receives a zero-length reply. Completion depends on protocol position, not simply on whether `read` returned zero somewhere.


</details>

## Derive the sender's obligation

The sender faces the same progress problem in reverse. A successful write can accept only a prefix of the requested bytes. Its invariant is that bytes before `offset` have been accepted by the local write operation and bytes at or after `offset` remain to be submitted. Retry an interrupted transfer without advancing; advance by the returned positive count; fail on other errors. Restarting from byte zero after a short write duplicates the prefix and corrupts the frame.

Both programs ignore `SIGPIPE`, allowing broken-connection writes to follow an error-return path instead of terminating through that signal. Successful writing does not prove that the peer application processed the request. The client therefore reads a reply and verifies both its length and its bytes. [Send interface and SIGPIPE](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/send.2.html)

This distinction matters when extending an echo protocol to operations with side effects. If the server performs an operation and the reply is lost, the client cannot infer from its read failure whether the operation happened. Retrying an echo is harmless; retrying a debit may require request identifiers and deduplication. A reliable stream does not, by itself, establish exactly-once application behavior.

## Choose the endpoint and its owners

Loopback confines the demonstration to the local machine. Binding port zero asks the kernel to choose an available port; `getsockname` retrieves it. Printing and flushing `PORT N` publishes the selected address to a supervising process. Merely calling `printf` would leave publication dependent on stdio buffering.

The listening descriptor owns the ability to accept a connection. `accept` produces a separate descriptor for that connection's stream. Both need cleanup. One connection is a deliberate bound on this experiment, not a claim that `listen(..., 1)` prevents every other connection attempt or defines a service-wide concurrency policy.

Before opening the implementations, sketch the server's resource ownership after socket creation, binding, accepting, and receiving a bad length. Your cleanup path must work from every reached state, including failure before a connection descriptor exists.

# Chapter 11 — Deriving an event loop from stalled work

Suppose two peers have work in progress. One has sent half a request and pauses; the other has sent a complete request. We want the second to progress without waiting for the first. We also require bounded memory, preservation of every accepted byte, and an explicit way to retire completed or failed operations.

The central problem is unfinished work. We need to remember enough about an operation to pause it safely, attend to something else, and resume it later. An event loop is one architecture for meeting that need. This chapter derives its state and scheduling rules, then studies them in a finite pipe experiment before applying the argument to sockets.

## Compare where waiting can live

One thread per connection lets ordinary blocking calls preserve progress on each thread's stack. This can be a reasonable, clear design at an appropriate scale, with bounds on thread count and resource use. One process per connection adds stronger address-space separation and a different resource cost. The application requirement is independence of progress; threads and event loops can both satisfy it under suitable assumptions. Actual latency, throughput, and complexity must be assessed for the workload.

## Derive the three kinds of result

A resumable transfer needs to distinguish progress, temporary inability to progress, and final completion or failure. A positive byte count advances state. A temporary result preserves it for later. EOF or a fatal error invokes the protocol's completion or failure rules.

POSIX supplies nonblocking operation through `O_NONBLOCK`. A read or write that would wait can return `EAGAIN` or `EWOULDBLOCK`; portable code checks both. These are interface contracts, not guesses based on how fast a previous call returned. To preserve existing file status flags, fetch them with `F_GETFL` and combine them with `O_NONBLOCK` when calling `F_SETFL`. Duplicated and inherited descriptors may share those status flags through the same open file description. [File-status flag contract](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man2/F_GETFL.2const.html)

Now we need a way to avoid repeatedly trying an operation that cannot progress. `poll` accepts descriptors and desired events, then reports readiness through returned event bits. `POLLIN` concerns reading; `POLLOUT` concerns writing. Readiness does not promise that an entire application frame can transfer. We still use nonblocking calls and interpret their results. [POSIX poll contract](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/poll.3p.html)

Notice the division of responsibility: `poll` decides when trying is useful; the transfer result determines what happened; the protocol decides what that result means. Confusing these layers is how “readable” becomes the false claim “a complete request exists.”

## Derive the persistent state

A framed connection needs a current phase and enough progress information to restart that phase:

| Phase | Information that must survive a handler return | Condition for advancing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Header | Four-byte storage and bytes collected | Header complete and length valid |
| Body | Validated length, storage, bytes collected | Declared body fully received |
| Reply | Output bytes and bytes already accepted | No output remains |
| Closing | Failure or completion reason and owned resources | Cleanup finishes |

For a reply of length `n`, maintain `0 <= sent <= n`. Only a positive write count changes `sent`; a temporary failure leaves it unchanged. A function-local offset that is discarded on return cannot satisfy this requirement. Neither can reusing the input buffer for the next request while the pending reply still refers to its bytes.

Suppose a socket write is asked to send 100 bytes, accepts 37, and the next attempt returns `EAGAIN`. Commit to the next buffer address and length before continuing: they are the original buffer plus 37 and 63 bytes. Returning to the dispatcher changes scheduling, not ownership of the remaining bytes.

Interest in events is itself derived state. Request `POLLOUT` while output remains. Remove that interest once the queue is empty. A socket may stay writable while we have nothing to send; continuously requesting write readiness can make the loop wake immediately and do no useful work.

## A smaller experiment with the same obligations

Our executable uses a pipe and two processes so we can inspect progress without also implementing connection management. The child generates exactly 65,536 bytes, with byte `i` equal to `i % 251`. The parent counts bytes and adds their values. Both pipe ends are nonblocking; buffers remain fixed in size.

The writer's state is `sent`, and its invariant is that the accepted prefix matches the specified sequence. The reader's state is a count and sum, with `count <= TOTAL_BYTES`. There is a separate lifecycle obligation: close unused pipe ends and reap the child. A stream ending is insufficient evidence that the producer completed successfully.

Before opening the code, sketch how the writer resumes after `EAGAIN` and how the reader recognizes final EOF. Include what happens if the child exits with failure after sending only a prefix.

<summary>Reference implementation</summary>


```c
#include <errno.h>
#include <fcntl.h>
#include <inttypes.h>
#include <poll.h>
#include <signal.h>
#include <stdint.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <sys/types.h>
#include <sys/wait.h>
#include <unistd.h>

enum { TOTAL_BYTES = 65536, CHUNK = 1024, WAIT_MS = 5000 };

static int nonblocking(int fd)
{
    int flags = fcntl(fd, F_GETFL);
    if (flags < 0)
        return -1;
    return fcntl(fd, F_SETFL, flags | O_NONBLOCK);
}

static int wait_ready(int fd, short events, short *returned)
{
    struct pollfd item = { .fd = fd, .events = events, .revents = 0 };
    int result;
    do {
        result = poll(&item, 1, WAIT_MS);
    } while (result < 0 && errno == EINTR);
    if (result == 0) {
        errno = ETIMEDOUT;
        return -1;
    }
    if (result < 0)
        return -1;
    *returned = item.revents;
    return 0;
}

static int produce(int fd)
{
    unsigned char buffer[CHUNK];
    size_t sent = 0;
    while (sent < TOTAL_BYTES) {
        size_t length = TOTAL_BYTES - sent;
        if (length > sizeof buffer)
            length = sizeof buffer;
        for (size_t i = 0; i < length; ++i)
            buffer[i] = (unsigned char)((sent + i) % 251);
        ssize_t n = write(fd, buffer, length);
        if (n > 0) {
            sent += (size_t)n;
            continue;
        }
        if (n < 0 && errno == EINTR)
            continue;
        if (n < 0 && (errno == EAGAIN || errno == EWOULDBLOCK)) {
            short events;
            if (wait_ready(fd, POLLOUT, &events) < 0)
                return -1;
            if (events & (POLLERR | POLLHUP | POLLNVAL)) {
                errno = EPIPE;
                return -1;
            }
            continue;
        }
        if (n == 0)
            errno = EIO;
        return -1;
    }
    return 0;
}

static int consume(int fd, size_t *count, uint64_t *checksum)
{
    unsigned char buffer[CHUNK];
    for (;;) {
        short events;
        if (wait_ready(fd, POLLIN, &events) < 0)
            return -1;
        if (events & (POLLERR | POLLNVAL)) {
            errno = EIO;
            return -1;
        }
        if (!(events & (POLLIN | POLLHUP)))
            continue;
        for (;;) {
            ssize_t n = read(fd, buffer, sizeof buffer);
            if (n > 0) {
                if ((size_t)n > TOTAL_BYTES - *count) {
                    errno = EOVERFLOW;
                    return -1;
                }
                for (ssize_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
                    *checksum += buffer[i];
                *count += (size_t)n;
            } else if (n == 0) {
                return 0; /* Only read(), not POLLHUP, establishes EOF. */
            } else if (errno == EINTR) {
                continue;
            } else if (errno == EAGAIN || errno == EWOULDBLOCK) {
                break;
            } else {
                return -1;
            }
        }
    }
}

int main(void)
{
    struct sigaction action = {0};
    action.sa_handler = SIG_IGN;
    if (sigemptyset(&action.sa_mask) < 0 ||
        sigaction(SIGPIPE, &action, NULL) < 0) {
        perror("ignore SIGPIPE");
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    int ends[2];
    if (pipe(ends) < 0) {
        perror("pipe");
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    if (nonblocking(ends[0]) < 0 || nonblocking(ends[1]) < 0) {
        perror("nonblocking");
        (void)close(ends[0]);
        (void)close(ends[1]);
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    pid_t child = fork();
    if (child < 0) {
        perror("fork");
        (void)close(ends[0]);
        (void)close(ends[1]);
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    if (child == 0) {
        (void)close(ends[0]);
        int result = produce(ends[1]);
        if (result < 0)
            perror("produce");
        if (close(ends[1]) < 0)
            result = -1;
        _exit(result == 0 ? EXIT_SUCCESS : EXIT_FAILURE);
    }
    (void)close(ends[1]);
    size_t count = 0;
    uint64_t checksum = 0;
    int result = consume(ends[0], &count, &checksum);
    if (result < 0)
        perror("consume");
    if (close(ends[0]) < 0) {
        perror("close reader");
        result = -1;
    }
    int child_status;
    pid_t waited;
    do {
        waited = waitpid(child, &child_status, 0);
    } while (waited < 0 && errno == EINTR);
    if (waited < 0) {
        perror("waitpid");
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    if (result < 0 || !WIFEXITED(child_status) ||
        WEXITSTATUS(child_status) != EXIT_SUCCESS || count != TOTAL_BYTES)
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    if (printf("bytes=%zu checksum=%" PRIu64 "\n", count, checksum) < 0 ||
        fflush(stdout) == EOF) {
        perror("stdout");
        return EXIT_FAILURE;
    }
    return EXIT_SUCCESS;
}

## Derive backpressure from conservation

Let `q` be queued reply bytes, `a` newly accepted reply bytes, and `d` bytes successfully sent. Across one transition, the accounting model is `q_next = q + a - d`. If accepted work repeatedly exceeds completed work, the queue grows. Faster allocation cannot change that relationship.

For a fixed memory budget `B`, the invariant is `0 <= q <= B`. Before accepting work, reserve enough capacity for the output it can require. When that capacity is unavailable, the design must defer input, reject work according to its protocol, or release other capacity. Unlimited buffering is incompatible with the stated bound.

One policy pauses reading at a high-water mark and resumes below a lower threshold. The gap reduces repeated toggling near one boundary. Another policy allows only one outstanding request per connection. That simplifies ownership and bounds but limits pipelining. Both are plausible choices; the required invariant does not dictate one exact threshold.

Per-connection bounds do not establish a global bound when connection count is unlimited. Account for the number of connections, each connection's fixed storage, and all queued bytes. Also consider dependency cycles: if two peers stop reading until their own pending writes finish, both can stall. Backpressure needs a protocol in which capacity can eventually be released.

Put numbers into that model before tuning the allocator. Suppose an initially empty reply queue has 64 KiB of capacity, accepted work produces 48 KiB of reply bytes per second, and sending completes 16 KiB per second. Under these sustained rates, the queue fills in two seconds: `64 / (48 - 16)`. Doubling capacity buys two more seconds; it does not permit those rates indefinitely. A bigger buffer could absorb a short burst, but sustained excess requires changing acceptance or completion rates.

The uncertain assumption is now specific: is the excess a bounded burst or an ongoing condition? Record queue growth during the relevant workload instead of starting with an allocator comparison. Separately, keep offering requests while a test peer pauses its reading until capacity is exhausted, then resume reading. Predict when input should pause and resume, and verify the memory bound and accepted bytes. For this sustained-load problem, a faster dispatcher helps only if it addresses the limiting work identified by the model.

## Separate safety from fairness and deadlines

The offsets and bounds protect data and memory. Progress additionally depends on giving operations opportunities to run. Draining one busy socket until `EAGAIN` can monopolize the loop if new input keeps arriving. Limit bytes or operations per connection per pass when many connections share the dispatcher. Our pipe experiment has one producer and a fixed total, so its drain loop is bounded by that total.

The sample's five-second timeout is per wait. An `EINTR` retry restarts that interval; repeated progress starts new waits too. It is not an absolute deadline for the process. A real request deadline can be represented as an expiration on a monotonic clock, with remaining time recalculated before each wait. Handling expiry is another state transition.

Regular files generally report ready; a subsequent storage operation may still incur latency. Lengthy computation, synchronous file I/O, or a blocking log destination can therefore delay every connection on an event thread. “Uses nonblocking sockets” is narrower than “every handler has bounded execution time.”

# Chapter 12 — Deriving experiments that can change your mind

You have two decisions to make. A streaming search sometimes misses a match near an input boundary, and you must decide whether a proposed fix addresses the cause. Someone also proposes a larger input buffer as a performance improvement, and you must decide whether the added resource cost is justified.

The goal is evidence strong enough for those decisions. The constraints are limited investigation time, tools that observe only part of execution, and a machine whose timing varies. Running more commands is useful only when their results can distinguish plausible explanations.

## Make the claim smaller than the uncertainty

For the suspected bug, the application contract says read boundaries must not alter search results. A model of a faulty implementation says it discards a partial match after each input chunk. An observation might be a missing match in one supplied file. The hypothesis is that discarding state caused that missing match. These are different statements: the contract tells us what should happen, the model suggests a mechanism, and the observation needs an explanation.

Write down the command, input bytes, expected output and status, actual output and status, and build identity. “Sometimes wrong” is not yet a reproducer. “Correct after adding a print” is an observation about an intervention, not proof that printing repaired the design.

For a candidate implementation, compare two plausible causes: state is lost at a chunk boundary, or a line-buffering design forgets to process its last unterminated line at EOF. A large file can exhibit both. Feed `nee` then `dle\n`, then separately supply `needle` without a newline and end the input. Different failures point toward different transitions.

Before checking the implementation, commit your predictions for each hypothesis. If either hypothesis could explain every possible outcome, it is too vague to guide this experiment.

## Derive a test from an invariant

The matcher's state summarizes the relevant prefix already consumed. Therefore feeding chunks `a` and `b` must agree with feeding their concatenation, provided no reset intervenes and the stream ends at the same point. The capstone reports when a match is found and needs no finalization function; a line-buffering alternative may need EOF processing. The property applies to either design.

Neither approach proves every input. Exhaustive splits are exhaustive only for the selected byte strings. A thousand random strings may never put a match across the boundary you care about.

Safety and progress also need separate evidence. Correct match output does not show that a program terminates when a pipe writer exits. A timeout detects a progress failure during a test, but does not explain whether a leaked descriptor, deadlock, or slow environment caused it. ## Derive a performance experiment

Our proposed change is a larger input buffer. The hypothesis is specific: when read calls commonly fill the current buffer, a larger request may reduce the number of calls needed for a fixed amount of input, reducing one component of elapsed time. It may also increase memory per active stream. If the input is a slow pipe, that mechanism may have little influence on completion time.

Estimate the opportunity before sweeping buffer sizes. Suppose a preliminary model divides a 100 ms run into 8 ms of overhead that fewer read calls might remove and 92 ms of remaining work. Even eliminating all 8 ms gives a 92 ms run, an 8% time reduction, provided the other work stays unchanged. A requested 25% reduction cannot come from that mechanism alone under this model. The arithmetic does not prove the breakdown: separating call overhead from waiting or data transfer is itself an experimental question.

Use that uncertainty to choose the next small experiment. First check whether larger requests actually reduce the call count for this input source; if they do not, the proposed explanation loses support. If they do, compare elapsed time to learn whether the removed calls matter. Also list repeated work whose result cannot change. A candidate design that rebuilds an unchanged search table for every chunk could instead reuse it, provided its inputs and lifetime justify reuse. Removing that work creates a different opportunity from accelerating its execution.

The experiment must keep the workload and success criteria comparable across variants. Otherwise a faster run might have searched fewer bytes, suppressed output, or failed early. First verify identical results and exit statuses. Then define the timing boundary: whole-process launch to exit, input transfer plus matching, or matching alone. These answer different questions.

## Check whether the measured work still exists

Consider a function with unsigned argument `n` that loops `n` times, incrementing a local counter of the same unsigned type from zero, then returns that counter. Its observable result is just `n`. A compiler may replace the loop with that result. If the counter has a narrower range, account for its wraparound instead of assuming the result still equals `n`. If the result is unused, an optimizer may remove still more work.

Before timing such a loop, predict what observable behavior requires the iterations to remain. A source-level loop count alone supplies no answer. This matters when comparing Chapter 9's mutex-protected counter with a private counter: the latter might be simplified far more aggressively, so the experiment would not isolate mutex cost.

Check generated code when a microbenchmark depends on specific work occurring. Choose representative input and consume a result meaningfully. Merely printing the final answer does not prevent closed-form simplification. Adding `volatile` can force a different set of accesses, but it changes the workload contract and does not automatically make the benchmark representative. Similarly, disabling optimization answers a question about an unoptimized build, not necessarily the production one.

These are alternative experimental designs with different boundaries. An end-to-end benchmark preserves the application context but mixes costs. A microbenchmark isolates a mechanism more closely but risks measuring an artificial workload. Use the narrower experiment to test a causal explanation, and the broader one to check whether the improvement matters to the user.

## Look for a counterexample to your favorite explanation

If a larger buffer improves a no-match file scan, “fewer system calls caused it” remains a hypothesis until supporting evidence distinguishes it from compiler differences, caching, or other changes. A useful follow-up records call counts in a separate diagnostic experiment, then compares timing without that tracing overhead. Likewise, equal-size files with different line and match patterns do not isolate I/O cost. Many matches add formatting and output, while the capstone can skip matching work after the first match on a line. Redirecting to `/dev/null` removes terminal rendering but not all formatting and write overhead. One changed file can alter several mechanisms at once.

Set stopping criteria before endlessly tuning. For the bug, require a passing regression, an invariant argument explaining the repair, and clean relevant checks. For optimization, define a workload-specific practical improvement and acceptable resource cost. If observed differences are smaller than the run-to-run variation, report inconclusive evidence rather than promoting the most favorable repetition. A rejected optimization can still be a successful investigation.

# Chapter 13 — Build and finish a streaming search tool

You need to find which lines of an input contain a literal pattern. The input can exceed memory; a single line can also exceed memory. Bytes may arrive in arbitrary groups, including a group ending halfway through the pattern. A caller needs a useful result even when a later file cannot be read, and a reliable indication that some work failed.

These constraints do not yet name an algorithm. Start by identifying information that a future decision needs. If a read ends with `nee` while the pattern is `needle`, forgetting that suffix makes the next `dle` indistinguishable from an unrelated beginning. Some information must survive the read. Whether you retain bytes, candidate positions, or a compact state is a design choice to investigate.

The final project, `sift`, combines an algorithmic argument with operating-system contracts. Derive them separately, then examine the boundary between them. A correct matcher does not make a failed output stream successful, and a reliable read loop does not prove that matching across chunks is correct.

## Establish what is fixed and what is chosen

| Claim | Its role in the design |
| --- | --- |
| Report each matching line once | Requirement; determines the observable events |
| Memory does not grow with input or line length | Requirement; rules out storing an arbitrarily long line |
| A positive read reports the bytes available from that call | External interface contract; the count determines the valid buffer range |
| Input contents do not form a guaranteed snapshot | Environment limit; results describe bytes actually observed |
| Patterns contain 1–4096 bytes and no newline | Chosen interface limit; simplifies matching and line semantics |
| Use an 8192-byte input buffer | Implementation choice; correctness must survive a different size |

Do not quietly turn the pattern limit or buffer size into a law of streaming search. A different interface could accept binary patterns from a file. A different algorithm could use a different representation of partial progress. Our task is to justify this particular set of promises and costs.

## Challenge the output requirement before choosing storage

Suppose the initial design says, “Allocate a growing buffer for each line, then print the line if it matches.” Before tuning allocation, ask why the line must be stored. Our caller needs the label and line number. Once a match is known, earlier line bytes cannot affect that report. Keeping the entire line is work introduced by the proposed design.

Change the promise to printing every matching line in full, with no bytes from nonmatching lines in the output. Now consider a nonseekable stream whose enormous first line has its only match near the end. Until that match appears, the program cannot know whether to emit the earlier bytes. Once it appears, it must reproduce them. A single pass therefore needs some way to retain an arbitrarily long prefix in the worst case. A more clever growth factor cannot remove that information requirement.

Possible revisions include a line-length limit, temporary-file storage, or rereading a stable seekable input. Each changes a resource or input assumption. Reporting line numbers removes the retention requirement only because it is the agreed output contract. Write down that agreement before celebrating the smaller buffer: removing necessary output would solve a different problem.

## Define the command before the algorithm

The command is:

```text
sift PATTERN [FILE ...]
```

`PATTERN` is a nonempty string of at most 4096 bytes, with no newline. There is no option parser: the first argument is always the pattern, even when it begins with a hyphen. A command-line argument cannot contain a zero byte, so this interface cannot express a pattern containing one. The input itself may contain any byte value.

With no files, the program reads standard input. A filename of `-` also means standard input. Other arguments name inputs opened for reading; the program does not write to them. Ordinary files are the expected use, but a named pipe may also be read and can wait for a producer. Repeating `-` reads the same standard-input stream from its current position, not a fresh copy.

Each matching line produces one output record:

```text
label:line-number
```

Line numbers start at 1. A newline byte ends a line. Carriage return is an ordinary byte, so CRLF input includes a carriage return just before each newline. A final line needs no newline to be searched. The program reports a line once even if the pattern occurs several times within it.

Labels are escaped byte by byte. A colon, backslash, control byte, or non-ASCII byte appears as `\xHH`. This keeps filenames containing colons or newlines from changing the output structure. It is a display format, not a Unicode-aware filename presentation. Standard input uses the fixed label `(stdin)`.

The result statuses are part of the interface:

| Status | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | At least one match, with no reported error |
| 1 | No matches, with no reported error |
| 2 | Invalid arguments or an input, output, allocation, or cleanup error |

An error takes precedence over matches. The program continues to later files after an input error, so partial results can still be useful. An output failure stops further searching once detected. Buffered output may reveal a failure only at the final flush.

## Choose what must survive a read

The operating system may divide `needle` into `nee` and `dle`. A matcher that starts over on each read misses that occurrence. The counterexample identifies the missing state; it does not by itself select a replacement algorithm.

Compare three useful candidates. Let `M` be pattern length and `L` the longest line encountered.

| Representation | Memory consequence | Reason to choose or reject it here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Read a whole line, then search it | Storage grows with `L` | Simple when line length is bounded; violates our arbitrary-line memory requirement |
| Retain up to `M - 1` previous bytes and search each joined window | Bounded by pattern and buffer sizes | Correct with careful duplicate suppression; a simple scan can repeatedly compare the same prefixes |
| Record matched-prefix length and a precomputed fallback table | Storage grows with `M` | Supports a linear-work matching argument without retaining previous input |

The suffix-window design is a legitimate solution to the memory requirement. We choose the third because it also gives a useful bound on matching work. Memory constraints motivate persistent state; the additional cost goal motivates how that state is represented.

At a byte-processing boundary before reporting a line, `matched` records the length of the **longest** pattern prefix that is also a suffix of the bytes examined on that line. For pattern `needle`, after input `xnee`, that length is 3. The next byte `d` increases it to 4. A mismatch may reduce it; a read boundary does not change it. This statement is the state invariant to preserve, not merely an interpretation of a variable name.

The matcher also needs the current line number and a Boolean saying whether that line has already been reported. Once a line is reported, further bytes on that line require only newline detection. A newline resets the prefix state and the reported flag, then advances the line counter. This deliberate reset prevents a pattern from spanning two lines.

The line counter uses `uintmax_t`. It is large, but it is still finite. The implementation rejects an increment that would overflow. Size limits are part of the contract even when reaching them would be impractical in an ordinary session.

## Reuse information after a mismatch

Consider pattern `ababac` and input `ababababac`. After matching `ababa`, the next input byte is `b`, but the next pattern byte would be `c`. Starting over discards information: the suffix `aba` already matches the first three bytes of the pattern.

A prefix table records this information in advance:

```text
pattern index:  0 1 2 3 4 5
pattern byte:  a b a b a c
prefix length: 0 0 1 2 3 0
```

Each table entry is the length of the longest proper prefix that is also a suffix of the pattern through that position. “Proper” means shorter than the whole sequence under consideration. If `k` bytes matched and the next byte disagrees, the next viable shorter candidate must itself be a suffix of those `k` bytes. It must also be a prefix of the pattern. That is precisely the relation the table records. Repeating the fallback considers progressively shorter candidates without rereading old input.

The argument explains why simply setting `matched` to zero on every mismatch is incomplete: a nonempty suffix may remain a valid beginning. It also explains why subtracting one is insufficient as a complete rule: not every shorter suffix is a pattern prefix. You could check those suffixes directly, but precomputing their relationships avoids repeating that work.

Trace the mismatch above. With five bytes matched, consult table entry 4 and keep three. The incoming `b` agrees with pattern position 3, bringing the matched length to four. The remaining input is `a,b,a,c`: the first `a` brings the length to five, the next `b` requires the same fallback and leaves four, and the final `a,c` bring it to five and then six. No previous input bytes need to remain in memory.

This is the central idea of the Knuth–Morris–Pratt family of string-matching algorithms. The useful property is that total matching work grows linearly with input length plus pattern length: each input step increases the matched length by at most one, and fallback steps reduce progress previously accumulated. Account for both increases and decreases across the whole input rather than multiplying a worst-looking inner loop by every byte. The table has one entry per pattern byte; its construction admits the same style of accounting.

This bound concerns matching, not all costs of running the command. Formatting and writing each label also takes work, and an output operation can wait on an external consumer. An algorithmic bound does not establish a wall-clock deadline.

Together, the pattern copy, prefix table, fixed 8192-byte input buffer, and small state use memory proportional to the pattern length. A line can be enormous without requiring a line-sized allocation. Output volume still has a cost; reporting millions of short matching lines is different work from finding one match on a huge line.

## Separate matching from operating-system work

The matcher never opens files or writes to standard output. It calls an application-supplied function when it finds a matching line. This separates a deterministic state machine from operations that can block or fail for external reasons.

The application creates one matcher, feeds it bytes, resets it between inputs, and destroys it once. The matcher copies the pattern, so it owns that memory independently of the caller. Input buffers are borrowed only during `matcher_feed` and must remain unchanged until it returns. A callback receives the line number by value and a caller-owned context pointer. It must not feed, reset, or destroy the active matcher: same-thread callback reentry can invalidate state or lifetime even without a thread data race. Calls on a different matcher are allowed.

```text
main owns matcher
    matcher owns pattern + prefix table
search_fd borrows matcher + descriptor
    read fills a temporary input buffer
    matcher_feed borrows bytes for one call
        callback borrows output context
main closes owned input descriptors and destroys matcher
```

The public header expresses the contracts that a caller needs. The structure definition stays private in `matcher.c`, allowing the representation to change without changing callers. After a callback or feed error, callers must reset or destroy the matcher; the API does not promise resumability after failure.

Before opening the interface, write down which failure leaves a resumable operation and which failure ends it. This reference chooses a simple rule: reset or destroy after a feed or callback failure. It does not attempt to undo output already accepted by the callback.

## Implement the state machine

The constructor validates the pattern before allocation. It checks each allocation immediately and preserves `errno` before cleanup. Successful library calls are not a safe place to keep a previous error code. Destruction accepts a null pointer, making constructor cleanup simple. The structure assignment from `(struct matcher){0}` establishes null pointer members explicitly through C initialization rules; zero-filled allocation alone is not a portable promise of null pointer representations. [C committee draft, initialization and `calloc`](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg14/www/docs/n1570.pdf).

The implementation stores pattern bytes without a terminating zero because every access is bounded by the recorded length. Its representation is a byte sequence, not a C string. Only the constructor accepts the caller's C-string representation.

Read the following source with the invariant beside you: outside a complete-match event, `matched` is smaller than the pattern length. When the lengths become equal, the code reports the line and resets `matched` before processing another byte. That prevents the next comparison from indexing beyond the pattern.

<details>
<summary>Reference implementation — capstone/matcher.c</summary>


```c
#include "matcher.h"

#include <errno.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

struct matcher {
    unsigned char *pattern;
    size_t *prefix;
    size_t length;
    size_t matched;
    uintmax_t line;
    int reported;
};

void matcher_destroy(struct matcher *m)
{
    if (m != NULL) {
        free(m->pattern);
        free(m->prefix);
        free(m);
    }
}

void matcher_reset(struct matcher *m)
{
    m->matched = 0;
    m->line = 1;
    m->reported = 0;
}

struct matcher *matcher_create(const char *pattern)
{
    if (pattern == NULL) {
        errno = EINVAL;
        return NULL;
    }
    size_t n = strlen(pattern);
    if (n == 0 || n > 4096 || strchr(pattern, '\n') != NULL ||
        n > SIZE_MAX / sizeof(size_t)) {
        errno = EINVAL;
        return NULL;
    }
    struct matcher *m = calloc(1, sizeof *m);
    if (m == NULL) {
        return NULL;
    }
    *m = (struct matcher){0};
    m->pattern = malloc(n);
    if (m->pattern == NULL) {
        int saved = errno;
        matcher_destroy(m);
        errno = saved;
        return NULL;
    }
    m->prefix = calloc(n, sizeof *m->prefix);
    if (m->prefix == NULL) {
        int saved = errno;
        matcher_destroy(m);
        errno = saved;
        return NULL;
    }
    memcpy(m->pattern, pattern, n);
    m->length = n;
    for (size_t i = 1, k = 0; i < n; ++i) {
        while (k > 0 && m->pattern[i] != m->pattern[k]) {
            k = m->prefix[k - 1];
        }
        if (m->pattern[i] == m->pattern[k]) {
            ++k;
        }
        m->prefix[i] = k;
    }
    matcher_reset(m);
    return m;
}

int matcher_feed(struct matcher *m, const unsigned char *data, size_t length,
                 match_callback emit, void *context)
{
    for (size_t i = 0; i < length; ++i) {
        unsigned char byte = data[i];
        if (byte == '\n') {
            if (m->line == UINTMAX_MAX) {
                errno = EOVERFLOW;
                return -1;
            }
            ++m->line;
            m->matched = 0;
            m->reported = 0;
            continue;
        }
        if (m->reported) {
            continue;
        }
        while (m->matched > 0 && byte != m->pattern[m->matched]) {
            m->matched = m->prefix[m->matched - 1];
        }
        if (byte == m->pattern[m->matched]) {
            ++m->matched;
        }
        if (m->matched == m->length) {
            m->reported = 1;
            m->matched = 0;
            if (emit(m->line, context) == -1) {
                return -1;
            }
        }
    }
    return 0;
}
```


</details>

## Connect the state machine to streams

The application has three jobs: interpret arguments, drive input, and translate matches into output records. `search_fd` owns no descriptor. That choice keeps resource cleanup with the code that decided whether a descriptor came from `open` or from standard input.

An interrupted read is retried, while a positive read is processed exactly once. A zero read finishes the input. Other errors return to the caller. The state survives across positive reads and resets only when a new input begins.

Output uses buffered standard I/O, which is convenient for small formatted records. The code checks formatting calls and the final flush. Ignoring `SIGPIPE` makes a closed output pipe visible as an I/O error instead of the default signal termination, allowing the tool to preserve its documented status 2.

The application uses a one-shot `close` check for an input it opened. If closing reports an error, it records failure and continues toward process termination; it does not blindly retry a possibly reused descriptor number. This is the short-lived program policy discussed in Chapter 3. A reusable library or long-running server would need a more explicit platform strategy.

The status-combination rule has its own invariant: once an error is recorded, later matches cannot turn the overall result into success. A match sets evidence that a matching line was found; the final output flush can still introduce an error. Keeping these facts separate prevents a last-file result from accidentally overwriting an earlier failure.

<details>
<summary>Reference implementation — capstone/sift.c</summary>


```c
#include "matcher.h"

#include <errno.h>
#include <fcntl.h>
#include <inttypes.h>
#include <signal.h>
#include <stdio.h>
#include <string.h>
#include <unistd.h>

struct output {
    const char *label;
    int found;
    int failed;
};

/* Escape labels so one result always occupies one unambiguous output line. */
static int print_label(const char *label)
{
    const unsigned char *p = (const unsigned char *)label;
    for (; *p != '\0'; ++p) {
        if (*p < 32 || *p >= 127 || *p == ':' || *p == '\\') {
            if (fprintf(stdout, "\\x%02x", (unsigned int)*p) < 0) {
                return -1;
            }
        } else if (fputc(*p, stdout) == EOF) {
            return -1;
        }
    }
    return 0;
}

static int report_match(uintmax_t line, void *context)
{
    struct output *out = context;
    if (print_label(out->label) == -1 ||
        fprintf(stdout, ":%" PRIuMAX "\n", line) < 0) {
        out->failed = 1;
        if (errno == 0) {
            errno = EIO;
        }
        return -1;
    }
    out->found = 1;
    return 0;
}

static int search_fd(int fd, struct matcher *m, struct output *out)
{
    unsigned char buffer[8192];
    matcher_reset(m);
    for (;;) {
        ssize_t n = read(fd, buffer, sizeof buffer);
        if (n > 0) {
            if (matcher_feed(m, buffer, (size_t)n, report_match, out) == -1) {
                return -1;
            }
        } else if (n == 0) {
            return 0;
        } else if (errno != EINTR) {
            return -1;
        }
    }
}

int main(int argc, char **argv)
{
    if (argc < 2) {
        fprintf(stderr, "usage: sift PATTERN [FILE ...]\n");
        return 2;
    }
    struct sigaction action = {0};
    action.sa_handler = SIG_IGN;
    if (sigemptyset(&action.sa_mask) == -1 ||
        sigaction(SIGPIPE, &action, NULL) == -1) {
        perror("sift: sigaction");
        return 2;
    }
    struct matcher *m = matcher_create(argv[1]);
    if (m == NULL) {
        perror("sift: pattern (1..4096 bytes, no newline)");
        return 2;
    }
    int had_error = 0;
    struct output out = {NULL, 0, 0};
    int inputs = argc > 2 ? argc - 2 : 1;
    for (int i = 0; i < inputs && !out.failed; ++i) {
        const char *path = argc > 2 ? argv[i + 2] : "-";
        int standard_input = strcmp(path, "-") == 0;
        int fd = STDIN_FILENO;
        out.label = standard_input ? "(stdin)" : path;
        if (!standard_input) {
            do {
                fd = open(path, O_RDONLY);
            } while (fd == -1 && errno == EINTR);
            if (fd == -1) {
                fprintf(stderr, "sift: %s: %s\n", path, strerror(errno));
                had_error = 1;
                continue;
            }
        }
        if (search_fd(fd, m, &out) == -1) {
            fprintf(stderr, "sift: %s: %s\n",
                    out.failed ? "stdout" : out.label, strerror(errno));
            had_error = 1;
        }
        /* No blind close retry: see the platform discussion in Chapter 3. */
        if (!standard_input && close(fd) == -1) {
            fprintf(stderr, "sift: close %s: %s\n", path, strerror(errno));
            had_error = 1;
        }
    }
    if (fflush(stdout) == EOF) {
        perror("sift: flush stdout");
        had_error = 1;
    }
    matcher_destroy(m);
    return had_error ? 2 : (out.found ? 0 : 1);
}
```

## State the limits of the progress argument

For a finite supplied chunk, the matcher advances through its bytes and each fallback decreases a bounded index. It terminates if the callback returns. That is a local progress argument with an explicit dependency.

The command has additional dependencies: blocking reads need input or EOF, and buffered output can eventually require a downstream reader. A pipe writer that never closes or a consumer that never reads can prevent completion. Bounded input memory and a linear-work matcher do not remove those waits. If the requirement changes to a service with deadlines, the I/O architecture and callback contract must change along with the timeout policy.

## Establish evidence for the contract

Run `make test`. The matcher-specific C test forces different chunk boundaries in the same data. The Python suite compares whole-program results with an independent byte-string oracle. It places matches around the 8192-byte input-buffer capacity, although pipe delivery does not force a particular read division. It also checks embedded zero bytes, long lines, the maximum pattern length, invalid patterns, input errors, escaped filenames, standard input, and broken output.

The tests do not make every failure physically happen. They do not exhaust available memory, simulate every filesystem, or prove the absence of races in an unrelated program. Their value comes from the connection between each case and a promised behavior. Read `tests/test_matcher.c` and `tests/check.py` as an executable companion to the contract.

The program is deliberately sequential. Input files can change while being read, so results describe the bytes observed rather than a coherent filesystem snapshot. Blocking input may wait indefinitely for more data. It has no recursive directory walk, regular expressions, Unicode case folding, or parallel execution. Those are specific possible extensions, each with design work of its own.

| Resource or operation | Ownership rule | Result to inspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `malloc`, `calloc` | Release the returned allocation with `free`; copied pointers do not create additional owners. | `NULL` indicates failure for the positive sizes used here. |
| `realloc(p, n)` with `n > 0` | On failure, retain ownership of `p`. On success, use the returned pointer and discard old aliases. | Assign through a temporary pointer so failure cannot lose `p`. |
| `open`, `pipe`, `socket`, `accept` | Close every owned descriptor reference, including unnecessary inherited copies. Descriptor zero can be valid. | Failure is `-1`; `pipe` places its two results in the supplied array. |
| `fopen`, `fclose` | `fclose` ends ownership of the stream and its descriptor. Mixing stream and descriptor I/O needs additional coordination. | Check opening and final flushing/closing, because buffered writes can fail late. |
| `opendir`, `readdir` | `closedir` releases the directory stream. A returned directory entry is borrowed; copy needed information before another read. | Distinguish end-of-directory from an error using the documented `errno` protocol. |
| `mmap`, `munmap` | Retain the original mapping address and length for release; descriptor and mapping lifetimes are separate. | Mapping failure is `MAP_FAILED`. |
| `fork`, `waitpid` | A created child gives the parent a responsibility to collect its status. | Handle three `fork` outcomes; decode wait status with the appropriate macros. |
| `pthread_create`, `pthread_join` | Keep argument storage alive while the thread uses it. Join each joinable thread, or deliberately detach it. | Pthread functions generally return an error number directly. |
| `getaddrinfo` | Release a successful result list with `freeaddrinfo`; pointers into it are borrowed. | Use its return code and `gai_strerror`; `EAI_SYSTEM` additionally refers to `errno`. |

The positive-size `realloc` rule is especially useful: an allocation failure can leave a fully usable old object. Design the caller's state update around that guarantee. [POSIX `realloc` specification](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/realloc.3p.html)

A retry policy belongs to an operation, not to a universal error-handling macro. A positive I/O result records progress; an interrupted call may require another attempt; `EAGAIN` in nonblocking code means wait for another opportunity. A failed `close` needs the platform-aware policy discussed in Chapter 3. After reporting failure, ask whether the remaining cleanup can block on a resource the program still owns.

For example, suppose your first pipe model predicts EOF when the producer exits. A parent that retains a write reference supplies a counterexample. After checking the pipe contract and tracing every reference, revise the claim: “The consumer reaches EOF after buffered bytes are consumed and every write reference is closed.” Your page should preserve the first prediction, explain why it failed, and derive a closure rule that covers both success and startup failure. The correction is evidence of reasoning progress, not something to erase from the record.

For every revision, keep three kinds of evidence distinct: what the interface promises, what this implementation assumes, and what your experiment actually observed. Record the operating system, compiler version, build flags, and relevant input sizes. When results differ across machines, preserve both observations before deciding which explanation needs changing.

For a first-principles review, choose one expensive design decision and reconstruct it without naming the current mechanism. A requirement such as “retain every accepted record until delivery” supports an argument; “use a queue” only names a candidate. Explain what information or work the requirement forces, what your implementation adds, and which small experiment would distinguish a necessary cost from an avoidable one.

Use the opening method's [six review questions](#know-when-to-revise-and-when-to-stop) on that explanation. Have a reviewer change one assumption without first showing you the reference answer. Record which conclusion survives, which must change, and the evidence for each. A complete checklist without a defensible answer to that variation is a reason to keep practicing.

Start with a precise question: “What does a zero result mean when the requested read length is positive?” Search the relevant interface name, then read its synopsis, return values, errors, description, and portability notes. Examples illustrate a contract; they rarely enumerate all of it.

```

## A source map

For **C language rules**, the examples request C17, associated with ISO/IEC 9899:2018. The freely accessible [WG14 N1570 committee draft](https://www.open-std.org/jtc1/sc22/wg14/www/docs/n1570.pdf) is a C11-era draft: it is useful for studying object lifetime, pointer arithmetic, and library terminology, but it is not the published C17 standard. Record its identifier when citing it, and investigate revision differences when exact wording matters.

For **portable operating-system interfaces**, use the [Open Group's POSIX.1-2017 preface and specification navigation](https://pubs.opengroup.org/onlinepubs/9699919799/frontmatter/preface.html). Read the edition explicitly. The book selects a POSIX.1-2008 interface baseline; a later specification can introduce requirements unavailable on an older target.

On **man7**, distinguish Linux implementation pages from reproduced POSIX specifications. For example, [`read(3p)`](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/read.3p.html) identifies itself as POSIX material and warns that Linux behavior may differ. A Linux-specific flag documented elsewhere on the site does not establish macOS support. Separate standards requirements, implementation notes, and historical behavior in your notes.

For **Clang and LLVM tools**, consult the [Clang user's manual](https://clang.llvm.org/docs/UsersManual.html), [AddressSanitizer documentation](https://clang.llvm.org/docs/AddressSanitizer.html), [UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer documentation](https://clang.llvm.org/docs/UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer.html), and [LLDB tutorial](https://lldb.llvm.org/use/tutorial.html). Match the documented feature to the installed tool's version. An instrumented run supplies evidence about the paths it executed; an uneventful run does not establish that every possible execution is valid.

## Compact glossary

| Term | Meaning in this book |
| --- | --- |
| Ownership | Responsibility for releasing a resource and controlling its lifetime. |
| Borrow | Permission to use a resource during an agreed interval without acquiring ownership. |
| Lifetime | Interval during which an object or resource remains valid for its permitted uses. |
| File descriptor | Process-local integer identifying a reference to an open resource. |
| Open file description | Kernel state behind descriptors, including a regular file's current offset. |
| EOF | The end-of-input condition defined by the particular stream or interface. |
| Partial I/O | Successful transfer of fewer bytes than requested. |
| Invariant | A property the design preserves at specified boundaries. |
| Data race | Conflicting unsynchronized accesses by threads, at least one a write, involving non-atomic data. |
| Undefined behavior | Behavior for which the C standard imposes no requirements. |
| Readiness | A report that some I/O can proceed without waiting; it may indicate EOF or an error. |
| Backpressure | A slow consumer causing an upstream producer to wait or limit queued work. |

## Use three forms of evidence

For each major skill, collect a **working artifact**, a **reasoned explanation**, and an **independent demonstration**.

A working artifact includes the implementation, build instructions, tests, and a precise contract. A reasoned explanation states ownership, invariants, failure behavior, and complexity or measured costs. An independent demonstration asks you to solve a variation without the reference implementation visible. These provide complementary evidence: code alone can hide misunderstandings, while an eloquent explanation can hide a lack of implementation practice.

Use four progress labels:

| Label | Observable evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Recognize | You can follow the explanation and predict a supplied example |
| Implement | You can build the specified behavior with documentation and explain your choices |
| Defend | You can justify correctness, handle counterexamples, and repair failures |
| Transfer | You can adapt the idea to an unfamiliar requirement without reconstructing the book's code from memory |

These are study labels, not hiring predictions. A useful goal is repeated evidence at Defend and Transfer for the core skills of your target role. Record assistance honestly so you can distinguish what you learned from what you already could do independently.

## Unit 1 — Algorithms as engineering decisions

Work on arrays, hash tables, heaps, balanced search structures, graph traversal, and binary search. For each, connect a complexity claim to a workload. Identify the state invariant before implementing a mutation. Explain the difference between expected, amortized, and worst-case cost.

**Build:** a merge utility for several sorted streams, followed by an index that supports insertion, lookup, and deletion. Begin with a simple reference implementation. Then choose a heap or hash-table design where it actually improves the stated workload. Include duplicates, empty inputs, extreme integer values, and adversarial insertion/deletion sequences.

**Completion evidence:** tests agree with an independent oracle; you explain why each operation preserves the invariant; a complexity argument counts the work the implementation actually performs. For example, requesting a small number of distinct values does not guarantee reading a small number of duplicate input records.

**Transfer task:** introduce a memory limit or change stable ordering requirements. Describe the resulting tradeoff before editing code. Learning a family of algorithms should make this conversation easier, rather than leave you searching for a memorized problem title.

## Unit 2 — Choose a language bridge

Keep C as a way to make machine and operating-system interfaces explicit. Choose one additional language from the role you intend to pursue. For a C++ role, add sustained practice writing idiomatic C++ rather than treating it as C with classes. Start with scope-based resource ownership, value semantics, references and views, move operations, standard containers, and exception guarantees. Add templates when a concrete abstraction benefits from them.

**Build:** a move-only descriptor owner and a small application that composes it with ordinary containers. Specify the empty state, move result, self-move policy, explicit close behavior, and how errors are reported. Exercise moving through a container, replacing an owned descriptor, and leaving a scope through an exception. A destructor's inability to report an ordinary return-value error should influence the interface.

**Completion evidence:** ownership is neither duplicated nor lost; borrowed references do not outlive their objects; a change to container capacity does not silently invalidate a view still in use; failure guarantees are stated and checked. Separate obtaining storage from establishing object lifetime. The C++ working draft's [object lifetime section](https://eel.is/c++draft/basic.life) is useful for investigating the distinction; match the standard edition to your chosen language mode.

**Transfer task:** replace a copying interface with a borrowed view. Explain precisely which caller behaviors become invalid, and decide whether that tradeoff is worth the saved copy. A faster interface with an unexplainable lifetime contract is unfinished work.

**Python alternative:** build a harness around a completed C program. Pass arguments without shell interpolation, enforce timeouts, check exit status, bound captured output, and retain inputs and results for replay. Add parsing, tests, and a documented environment. Explain which resource owns each subprocess and how cancellation reaches it. Appendix F extends the experiment and result-accounting practice; a later ML path adds framework and numerical knowledge.

**Rust alternative:** when a target role uses Rust, port one ownership-focused component. Explain moves, borrowing, error results, and thread-sharing constraints in its terms. Avoid translating C pointer patterns mechanically. The completion standard remains a clear contract, meaningful failure tests, and an independent variation. Learning every language at once is not a prerequisite for progressing through the other units.

## Unit 3 — Follow an operation through the machine

Connect source code to generated instructions, caches, address translation, faults, and scheduling. Learn enough assembly for the architecture you use to inspect a small optimized function. Ask which work happens once, once per element, once per cache line, or once per page. Do not assume that two machines share a cache-line size or a memory-access cost.

**Build:** a set of experiments that performs equivalent logical work with contiguous storage and dependent pointer traversal. Vary the working set and traversal order. Keep data generation outside the measured operation, preserve an observable result, and inspect generated code to check whether the intended work remains.

**Completion evidence:** you can distinguish an observation from its proposed mechanism. A slower traversal might involve dependency chains, prefetch behavior, cache locality, or address translation; elapsed time alone does not identify the cause. Provide repeated measurements, machine/compiler details, and evidence that supports or limits your interpretation.

**Transfer task:** predict an intervention, run it, and explain a neutral or contrary result. The successful outcome is a better-supported explanation, not a requirement that every attempted optimization wins.

## Unit 4 — Operating systems under observation

Use the process and mapping examples to study address spaces, copy-on-write, page faults, system calls, scheduling, and descriptor inheritance. Connect each concept to an observation and identify the limits of the observation. Distinguish the language contract from the kernel implementation and the filesystem behavior.

**Build:** an experiment notebook that traces a small process tree and records what changes when a process allocates, touches, forks, and modifies memory. Use a dedicated Linux environment for Linux-specific tracing and profiling; keep portable experiments runnable on macOS. A Linux-specific command is not evidence about macOS internals.

**Completion evidence:** you can explain why allocating an address range and touching its pages are different events, describe how parent and child data evolve after `fork`, and account for every inherited descriptor in a pipeline. Record measured values without treating them as portable constants.

**Transfer task:** diagnose a process that seems to hang despite having little CPU usage. Construct competing hypotheses involving input, a retained pipe writer, a lock, and a child that has not terminated. Choose observations that distinguish those hypotheses.

Linux performance tools may have environment-specific access restrictions. Consult the kernel's [performance-event documentation](https://docs.kernel.org/admin-guide/perf-security.html) when a measurement is unavailable; report that limitation instead of inventing a counter value.

## Unit 5 — Concurrency with a correctness argument

First implement a bounded queue using mutexes and condition variables. Specify what close means, which items are accepted, whether accepted items drain, and who joins the workers. Test capacity one: it forces transitions that a generous queue can hide.

**Build:** a worker pipeline with multiple producers and consumers, an explicit close operation, and startup-failure cleanup. Make each accepted item observable exactly once within a completed in-process run. That is a defined local property; it is not a claim about crash recovery or distributed delivery.

**Completion evidence:** every shared field has a synchronization rule; wait predicates are explicit; close wakes every class of waiter; state and synchronization objects outlive their users. Give the argument before citing a clean race-detector run. The [POSIX condition-wait specification](https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man3/pthread_cond_wait.3p.html) explains the wait/mutex relationship your reasoning relies on.

**Transfer task:** after the mutex version is correct and measured, investigate a single-producer/single-consumer ring. Write down publication and reuse ordering, justify each atomic operation, and identify where your proof relies on having exactly one producer and one consumer. The C++ working draft's [data-race and synchronization rules](https://eel.is/c++draft/intro.races) are a reference for that investigation. Lock-free algorithms are an advanced extension, not a substitute for the queue's contract.

## Unit 6 — Services with resource budgets

Extend the framed protocol into a service with several simultaneous clients. Every connection needs persistent parsing and output state, a memory budget, a lifetime owner, and a deadline policy. Define how peer closure interacts with a reply that has not yet been sent.

**Build:** a framed echo or event service with a bounded number of connections, limited pending output per connection, and explicit overload behavior. One slow reader must not stall independent clients or grow memory without a bound. A client that sends a few bytes periodically must not extend an absolute request deadline indefinitely.

**Completion evidence:** deterministic tests fragment headers and bodies, stop clients from reading, close during each protocol phase, and verify cleanup. You can explain the per-connection state machine, fairness policy, and total-memory bound. Document where disk access or computation can still block the loop.

**Transfer task:** change the protocol to allow multiple requests on a connection. Explain what request/reply ordering means and what prevents unbounded queued replies. Merely adding an outer loop to the blocking example does not answer either question.

**Production extension:** let a client lose its connection after submitting work but before receiving acknowledgement. The client cannot infer whether the work executed. Define a request identifier, duplicate policy, retention bound, and recovery behavior before adding retries. A local queue's single-consumption property does not imply exactly-once effects after a crash. Then design a compatible protocol change: specify which metrics detect trouble during rollout and what rollback can restore, including whether new stored data remains readable. These are further design and implementation tasks, not properties supplied by the chapter's socket example.

## Unit 7 — Performance claims that survive questions

Correctness and measurement belong together. Give the benchmark a workload, an arrival model, and an observable result. Distinguish throughput from latency. For latency, state where a sample starts and ends, how waiting is counted, and whether overload causes rejection, queueing, or omission from the measurements.

**Build:** a reproducible benchmark harness for one of your completed programs. Report sample counts and a distribution, including tail behavior when enough observations support it. A client that waits for each reply before sending another request is a particular workload; it does not reproduce an externally imposed arrival stream.

**Completion evidence:** another person can reproduce the setup and understand its limits. Change one factor at a time. Preserve the same answers and failure behavior after optimization. Do not present nanosecond precision simply because a clock API exposes nanosecond units.

**Transfer task:** review an attractive performance claim and identify what evidence is missing. Rewrite it as a narrower statement supported by the actual experiment.

## Unit 8 — Integrate and explain a complete system

Choose one larger project. Both are study vehicles with explicit limits; neither reproduces a firm's production system.

**Event replay:** write a small synthetic event format containing a sequence number, an instrument identifier, a price, and a quantity. Specify widths, byte order, valid ranges, and the policy for duplicates or missing sequence numbers before implementing the decoder.

Build first a sequential reference model and parser; then a state index and reproducible snapshots; then optional bounded concurrency; finally measured improvements. Keep the original reference model as an oracle. A reproducible synthetic data generator makes malformed and out-of-order cases testable without access to market data.

**Local bounded job service:** begin with stable synthetic inputs and a deterministic worker whose answers can be checked independently. Add admission limits, a bounded queue, request identifiers, explicit job states, deadlines, cancellation, and shutdown. Retain results under a stated memory bound. Specify what survives a restart; persistence is an additional feature to design and test, not an implication of accepting a request. A simulator can explore scheduling before real subprocess workers add lifecycle failures.

Use [Appendix F](#appendix-f--from-c-systems-to-ai-infrastructure) for worked reasoning about fixtures, scheduling, and evaluation records. Keep queue delay, service time, end-to-end latency, rejection, timeout, and incorrect-result counts distinct. Report the denominator behind every rate. The fixture measures infrastructure behavior; a claim about model quality requires a real model, appropriate data, and justified evaluation methods.

**Completion evidence:** the repository contains a protocol contract, ownership and state diagrams, independent correctness tests, failure tests, a benchmark report, and a short design review. Explain an abandoned approach and the evidence that caused you to abandon it. Be able to make a requirement change while preserving the relevant tests.

**Transfer task:** present the design in five minutes, then accept a change such as a lower memory budget or a slower consumer. Explain the consequences, ask clarifying questions, and implement a small part of the adjustment. Communication should make the engineering easier to inspect.

## Task 1 — Merge without hiding the cost

**First attempt: 45–60 minutes.** Write C that accepts `k` sorted arrays of `int64_t` and emits the first `m` distinct values of their union in increasing order. Inputs are immutable and individually sorted; empty arrays are valid. An empty array may have a null pointer. Do not concatenate and sort the whole input.

For arrays `[-3, 1, 1, 8]`, `[-3, 0, 8, 9]`, and `[]`, with `m = 5`, emit:

```text
-3 0 1 8 9
```

With `m = 3`, emit `-3 0 1`; with `m = 0` or no arrays, emit nothing. If fewer distinct values exist, emit all of them. Define an output callback that accepts a value or reports failure. Stop on failure, release owned memory, and return an error.

Target `O(k)` auxiliary memory and `O(k + R log(k + 1))` work, where `R` is the number of input elements examined. Duplicates mean `R` can exceed `m`. Write the invariant before the implementation.

Rubric:

- Correct ordering, deduplication, stopping, and extreme values including `INT64_MIN` and `INT64_MAX`.
- An invariant explaining why the next emitted value is safe to choose.
- A bound based on consumed elements, with no hidden full-input materialization.
- An independent simple oracle, adversarial duplicate cases, and recorded error behavior.

## Task 2 — Repair unfamiliar code from its contract

**First attempt: 60–75 minutes.** This intentionally flawed excerpt collects length-prefixed frames. Its bugs are not marked. Build your own harness and repair it without replacing the task with an unrelated implementation.

The header is four bytes, most significant byte first. Bodies may contain any bytes and have lengths 0–4096. The callback borrows a complete body only during the call and cannot fail. Input chunks do not alias the buffer. A zero-length input may use a null pointer. Initialize `struct frames` to zero and eventually free its `data` field.

Required behavior: emit complete frames exactly once, preserve incomplete input across calls, reject an oversized length as soon as its header is available, and preserve the previous buffer on allocation failure. Return zero for accepted input and minus one for an error. After malformed input, the caller may destroy the object. At EOF, remaining buffered bytes mean truncation.

```c
#include <stddef.h>
#include <stdlib.h>
#include <string.h>

struct frames {
    unsigned char *data;
    size_t used, capacity;
};
typedef void (*emit_frame)(const unsigned char *, size_t, void *);

int frames_feed(struct frames *f, const unsigned char *src, size_t n,
                emit_frame emit, void *context)
{
    if (n == 0) return 0;
    size_t needed = f->used + n;
    if (needed > f->capacity) {
        f->data = realloc(f->data, needed);
        if (f->data == NULL) return -1;
        f->capacity = needed;
    }
    memcpy(f->data + f->used, src, n);
    f->used = needed;
    while (f->used >= 4) {
        size_t length = ((size_t)f->data[0] << 24) |
                        ((size_t)f->data[1] << 16) |
                        ((size_t)f->data[2] << 8) | f->data[3];
        if (f->used < 4 + length) return 0;
        emit(f->data + 4, length, context);
        f->used -= 4 + length;
        memcpy(f->data, f->data + 4 + length, f->used);
    }
    return 0;
}
```

The byte sequence `00 00 00 03 61 62 63 00 00 00 00 00 00 00 02 00 ff` must emit bodies `61 62 63`, empty, and `00 ff`, regardless of chunk divisions. Feed `00 00 10 01` alone to test rejection before a body arrives. Use an allocation wrapper to force a failed growth request; do not try to exhaust your machine.

Rubric:

- Reproducers establish actual violations of the stated contract.
- Explanations identify the violated lifetime, range, or progress invariant.
- Repairs preserve ownership and already buffered input on allocation failure.
- Regression tests cover chunk divisions, malformed input, EOF, and sanitizer execution.

## Task 3 — Close a queue while people are waiting

**First attempt: 75–90 minutes.** Implement a bounded queue with two producers and two consumers using pthread mutexes and condition variables. Define `push`, `pop`, and `close`. Closing rejects future pushes, preserves accepted items for draining, and wakes every waiter. A closed, empty queue makes `pop` report completion.

Producers submit disjoint IDs, such as 0–999 and 1000–1999. In a normal run, consumers collectively receive each ID exactly once. In a run closed early, every successful push must be received exactly once; rejected pushes must not appear. Record acceptance as part of the test oracle. Test capacity 1 as well as a larger capacity.

In the normal run, a coordinator joins both producers, closes the queue, then joins the consumers. For early-close cases, use barriers or test hooks to establish that the intended wait state has been reached before closing; an arbitrary sleep does not establish it. A startup-failure path must close and wake the queue before joining workers that could otherwise remain blocked.

Rubric:

- Queue state transitions preserve bounds and exactly-once delivery.
- Every wait has a protected predicate; wakeups are never treated as stored permissions.
- Closure works with blocked producers and consumers, without using sleeps as synchronization.
- Every started thread is joined and resources remain valid through cleanup, including partial startup failure.

Do not require a deterministic order between unrelated producers unless your contract explicitly defines one. Stress tests support the argument; they do not replace it.

## Task 4 — Keep a slow peer from owning the service

**Initial design: 45 minutes; implementation lab: 2–4 hours.** Build a loopback TCP service using `poll` and nonblocking connected sockets. Reuse Chapter 10's four-byte length header and 4096-byte maximum. Support several frames per connection and at least two simultaneous clients. Declare a finite connection limit.

Allow at most one pending reply per connection; stop reading another request until that reply drains. Interpret EOF between frames as normal, EOF inside a frame as truncation, and a write-half-close following a complete frame as compatible with receiving its reply. Give each request an absolute monotonic deadline from its first header byte through its completed reply, rather than restarting a timeout after every byte.

First smoke-test a client sending `00 00 00 03 63 61 74`, which must receive exactly that header and body. Then run client A in a separate worker that sends many maximum-size frames without reading replies, while client B repeats the small request. Confirm through instrumentation or an injected I/O harness that A actually exercises pending output and would-block handling; one small reply may fit entirely in kernel buffers. If you constrain socket buffers, do not assume the requested size is their exact effective capacity. The test controller must remain runnable if A's sending blocks. Add fragmented headers, zero-length frames, oversized lengths, and a peer that sends one header byte and stops.

Rubric:

- Explicit states and offsets handle positive progress, interruption, would-block, EOF, and failure.
- Connection count and buffered-byte bounds remain true under slow readers.
- Other clients progress, expired requests are closed, and idle writable sockets do not cause busy spinning.
- Automated peers verify byte equality, half-close behavior, deadlines, and eventual resource cleanup.

Use bounded test timeouts as watchdogs. Do not mistake their termination of a hung server for successful server deadline handling.

## Task 7 — Accept one result when work repeats

**Optional systems lab: 90–120 minutes.** Build an in-memory coordinator for logical jobs and execution attempts. A job ID identifies one immutable payload and computation version. Resubmitting that identity refers to the existing job; reusing its ID with different payload bytes or version is a conflict. Do not treat an unchecked hash match as proof of identity.

A timeout may launch another attempt while the original still runs. # Appendix F — From C systems to AI infrastructure

A model service still owns buffers, accepts work, waits for resources, handles interrupted communication, and produces evidence about its behavior. The C chapters give you a way to reason about those obligations. The next step is to change the workload while keeping the discipline: define success, derive necessary state, challenge an assumption, and measure the remaining uncertainty.

This appendix develops three designs that connect Chapters 9–13 to AI infrastructure. They are worked arguments and lab specifications; the repository does not supply their implementations. Begin with deterministic simulated workers on your CPU. No paid API, model download, or accelerator is required. Appendix E Tasks 7–9 ask you to implement related contracts independently.

## Derive admission from retained memory

Suppose a service receives jobs containing an immutable input, a model version, and a deadline. A worker eventually returns a bounded result. Before choosing a thread count, answer a more basic question: what permits the service to accept another job?

For this study design, acceptance means the service has validated the request and reserved its maximum accounted memory. A successful submission is distinct from successful computation. Rejection transfers no ownership. Accepted jobs eventually receive a terminal outcome if workers and the coordinator continue making progress; a deadline supplies a failure policy, not evidence that computation has stopped.

Assume a 64 MiB accounting budget, including 16 MiB reserved for fixed state. Each accepted job reserves 6 MiB covering its bounded input, metadata, working storage, and retained output. Assume these bounds are enforced and no additional allocation escapes the ledger. Then:

```text
fixed reservation + sum of live job reservations <= budget
16 MiB + n * 6 MiB <= 64 MiB
n <= 8
```

This is a bound on the declared accounting model. It is not a measurement or guarantee of process resident memory; stacks, allocator behavior, and libraries must be accounted for before making that larger claim. A real worker whose workspace is unknown invalidates the premise. Reducing a thread count does not by itself establish a memory bound.

Reserve before acceptance, under the same synchronization that changes the queue. Checking available space and updating it separately allows two submitters to spend the same capacity. Queued jobs retain reservations too. Otherwise eight running jobs plus an unbounded waiting list defeats the calculation.

Keep the accounting rule attached to ownership: memory becomes available when no owner can still access it. A cancellation request can remove a queued job, but a running worker may still hold its input. Marking that job cancelled and immediately reusing its storage introduces a lifetime bug. Separate the reported outcome from acknowledgement that execution and buffer access have ended. Completed replies also retain capacity until delivered or disposed of according to policy.

**Try a counterexample:** all eight slots are occupied when the oldest worker exceeds its deadline. A ninth request arrives. Under which event can its reservation succeed?

<details>
<summary>Review the capacity transition</summary>


Deadline expiry alone releases nothing that the worker still owns. The ninth request must remain unaccepted or be rejected until enough ownership actually ends. If cancellation never stops the worker, reserved capacity can remain unavailable. A design that requires recovery from that condition needs an enforceable termination or isolation mechanism and a cleanup contract. Renaming the state does not supply one.


</details>

## Derive batching from a cost model

Batching groups several jobs into one worker invocation. It can reduce repeated setup work, but gathering a batch consumes time. Whether it helps depends on both the worker cost and the arrival pattern.

Use this deliberately simulated worker duration:

```text
T(b) = 4 ms + 2 ms * b, for batch sizes 1 through 4
```

There is one worker, jobs have equal size, and all members receive their results at batch completion. Ignore transfer and scheduling overhead. These are invented simulation parameters, not CPU or GPU performance measurements. Four individual jobs cost 24 ms of worker service; one batch of four costs 12 ms. Under a continuously supplied backlog, the modeled service capacities are approximately 167 versus 333 jobs per second. Neither number predicts request latency.

Now submit four jobs at simulated times 0, 3, 6, and 9 ms. Compare immediate single-job service with collecting four jobs, allowing at most 9 ms from the oldest queued arrival:

| Policy | Completion times, in arrival order | Latencies from arrival | Mean latency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Single jobs | 6, 12, 18, 24 ms | 6, 9, 12, 15 ms | 10.5 ms |
| One batch of four, starting at 9 ms | 21, 21, 21, 21 ms | 21, 18, 15, 12 ms | 16.5 ms |

The batch uses less worker time and finishes the whole set earlier, while increasing mean latency. A 15 ms deadline from each arrival would also change which jobs succeed. State how equality at the deadline is treated before testing it. Batching is a tradeoff you can derive; a larger batch is not automatically a better service.

Give the scheduler a maximum batch size, a maximum wait measured from the oldest queued job, and absolute job deadlines. A new arrival must not reset the oldest job's wait. Before dispatch, expire queued work according to the contract and reserve any batch-specific workspace. In this toy model, workspace is already covered by the per-job reservations; another implementation must justify that assumption separately.

Start with FIFO selection and record its consequences. A large or slow job can delay smaller jobs; selecting only cheap jobs can starve expensive ones. Change one policy at a time. Measure waiting, service, completed-job latency, rejections, cancellations, and deadline failures on the same arrival trace. A benchmark that submits the next request only after a reply measures a different workload from an externally scheduled arrival stream.

**Exercise:** change the arrivals to 0, 20, 40, and 60 ms. Predict both policies with the 9 ms batching wait before simulating them. Explain why the previous saturated-capacity calculation does not describe this trace.

<details>
<summary>Review sparse arrivals</summary>


Immediate service completes each job after 6 ms. Each batching timer expires without collecting another job, so each job waits 9 ms and then takes 6 ms: 15 ms latency. There is no shared setup saving. The arrival rate, rather than the saturated worker capacity, limits throughput here.


</details>

## A lost reply changes what the caller knows

The worker finishes a request, but its reply disappears when a connection closes. A client timeout cannot distinguish that history from one in which computation never began. Retrying may be necessary for progress and may repeat execution. Derive the identity you need before writing a retry loop.

Distinguish three objects:

| Object | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Logical job | One requested computation, identified across retries |
| Attempt | One dispatch of that computation, with its own attempt identifier |
| Accepted result | The single result the coordinator commits for the logical job |

Use a bounded, single-coordinator study design whose guarantee lasts for that coordinator's lifetime. The job record binds an ID to canonical input bytes, model version, and computation settings. The same ID with different contents is a conflict. Matching retries attach to the existing job or retrieve its stored terminal outcome. Bound record and result storage; when full, reject new logical jobs. This initial design retains records until the run ends, making the cost of deduplication explicit.

Combining this design with the scheduler changes its budget: the earlier 6 MiB reservation assumed one execution per job. Reserve each overlapping attempt's workspace separately and retain that charge until it stops. Cached results and deduplication records remain charged after a reply is delivered; delivery does not end their ownership.

If attempts may overlap, give every attempt a unique ID. Serialize the transition from unresolved to terminal. A successful attempt can commit only while the logical job remains unresolved. Later successes cannot overwrite it; their buffers still need cleanup. A retry limit or deadline may instead commit failure, after which a late success remains late. Decide that policy before an ambiguous history occurs.

The invariant is **at most one accepted result per logical job**, not exactly one execution. A deterministic worker makes repeated answers easy to compare, but determinism alone does not suppress repeated work. For a stochastic worker, the accepted answer may depend on which attempt wins. Pinning a model version and settings gives the request meaning without promising identical outputs.

**Exercise:** attempt A finishes, the coordinator commits its answer, the reply is lost, and the client resubmits. Then repeat the trace with the coordinator crashing immediately after the commit. Which retained fact makes the histories different?

<details>
<summary>Review the restart boundary</summary>


During one lifetime, the job record lets the coordinator return its existing outcome. After restart, an in-memory record is gone. Continuing the guarantee requires durable identity and outcome state, an atomic commit rule, and recovery that restores that state before accepting retries. Committing a result durably before publishing success closes one window; a crash before that commit can still require repeated computation. Durable deduplication does not promise exactly-once execution.

External side effects add another boundary. If a worker changes another service and crashes before recording that change, a local result log alone cannot decide whether repeating the effect is safe. The study worker computes a result without external effects. Extending that contract requires coordination with the destination. Retention and expiry also matter: deleting deduplication records limits the period for which retries can recover old outcomes.


</details>

## Evaluation is an accounting system too

A fast runner can still produce a misleading experiment. Begin with a manifest describing every intended case: stable ID, input, expected output or scoring rule, provenance, and split membership. Record generator version and seed for synthetic fixtures; record permitted source and preprocessing for external data. Keep model version, configuration, code revision, environment, and the exact manifest with each run.

Start with a deterministic baseline whose answers are independently checkable, such as the capstone's byte-pattern predicate compared with a simple whole-input oracle on bounded fixtures. This establishes runner behavior before adding model uncertainty. A file of canned successful replies cannot test timeout accounting or retry behavior. Add injected failures and preserve an outcome for every intended case.

Suppose 100 cases are submitted. Ninety complete, of which 80 satisfy the scoring rule; ten time out. Then `80/90` is approximately 88.9% correctness among completed cases. If the declared end-to-end success rule requires a correct answer before timeout, `80/100` is 80% success over submissions. Report both denominators and the ten timeouts. Removing missing results silently changes the question. Do not invent completion latencies for timed-out cases; report their count and observation deadline separately from the distribution conditional on completion.

Distinguish test harness correctness from model quality. Exact byte equality can judge the parser fixture. It may not judge a task with multiple valid answers. Define the scoring rule before comparing candidates and inspect disagreements. If human judgment is needed, preserve the rubric and adjudication procedure. A larger aggregate score cannot explain whether changed behavior came from better answers, easier cases, or dropped failures.

When a model or threshold is fitted, separate training, development, and final held-out evaluation data. Training changes parameters; development informs choices; final evaluation estimates behavior on examples withheld from those choices. Split related examples together when shared origins would leak information. Repeatedly selecting changes from the final set turns it into development data, whatever the filename says.

**Exercise:** optimization lowers mean completed-job latency while doubling timeouts. Write two possible explanations and one trace that separates them. Then change the workload to longer inputs without changing the original report. Identify precisely which conclusions no longer have supporting evidence.

## Optional bridge: a tiny learned worker

For an ML-focused path, add Python and basic linear algebra after the deterministic service works. Learn array shapes, dtypes, indexing, exceptions, environments, and tests. Follow the official [PyTorch basics sequence](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/basics/intro.html) for tensors, data loading, model construction, differentiation, and saved parameters. Keep the first model small enough to train and evaluate on the CPU.

For example, generate two-dimensional points and label them by a documented linear rule. Use fixed, separate training, development, and evaluation manifests. A linear classifier can transform input shape `[batch, 2]` into two class scores per example, shape `[batch, 2]`. Labels have shape `[batch]`. Derive those dimensions before selecting a layer; a tensor that broadcasts successfully can still express the wrong calculation. The known label rule supplies an oracle for the generator, while a constant-class predictor supplies a simple quality baseline.

Training computes predictions and a loss, obtains gradients, and updates parameters. Gradients accumulate unless reset: explain where `optimizer.zero_grad()`, `loss.backward()`, and `optimizer.step()` belong in your loop. Inference uses the chosen parameters to produce predictions without a training update. [PyTorch's optimization tutorial](https://docs.pytorch.org/tutorials/beginner/basics/optimization_tutorial.html)

Treat evaluation behavior and gradient recording as separate controls. `model.eval()` changes behavior in modules such as dropout; it does not itself disable gradient recording. An appropriate `torch.inference_mode()` context disables autograd work and imposes restrictions on tensors created inside it, but does not put the model into evaluation mode. Check both contracts before using them together. [Module evaluation mode](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.nn.Module.html#torch.nn.Module.eval), [Inference mode](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/generated/torch.autograd.grad_mode.inference_mode.html)

Record seeds and software versions, and check repeated runs in the stated environment. A seed does not guarantee identical results across releases, platforms, or CPU and GPU execution. Preserve that uncertainty in the report. [PyTorch reproducibility notes](https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/notes/randomness.html)

Only after choosing a specialization should you add accelerator-specific work: tensor layouts and kernels, device-memory ownership, synchronization, numerical precision, profiling, or distributed training. These require further study and suitable hardware for credible measurements. The CPU labs establish scheduler, failure, and experiment reasoning; they do not establish accelerator performance expertise.
